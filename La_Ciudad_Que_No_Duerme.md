# La Ciudad Que No Duerme

## Prologue: The Last Good Night

There is a story they used to tell children in Vísperas, back when children could still be put to bed.

It went like this. Long before the city had a name, before the harbor was dredged and the hills were terraced and the first stone was laid, there was a girl who could not stop dreaming. She dreamed so hard and so deep that her dreams left her body and went walking, and where they walked the ground grew soft and dark and restful, and travelers who lay down there slept the best sleep of their lives and woke healed of things they had not known were wounds.

People came from far away to sleep on that ground. They built shelters, then houses, then streets. They built a city on the girl's dreaming, the way you might build a house over a warm spring, and they made a bargain with her without ever quite saying it aloud: *you dream for us, and we will keep you safe while you do it.* She took the city's nightmares into her sleep so the city would not have to carry them. Every bad dream that should have belonged to a butcher or a banker or a frightened child, she dreamed instead, alone, so that the rest of them could close their eyes and fall clean into the dark.

They called her *la Durmiente*. The Sleeper. And for a very long time the bargain held, and Vísperas was famous, across three coastlines, as the city where everyone slept well.

That is the story. Grandmothers told it to make small children behave. *Duérmete,* they'd say, *o despertarás a la que duerme por todos.* Sleep, or you'll wake the one who sleeps for all of us.

Nobody tells it anymore, because there is no one left to tell it to. In Vísperas the children do not sleep. Neither do their parents, nor the old, nor the sick, nor the newborn who arrive already staring. For eleven years now the whole city has been awake — every window lit, every street busy at four in the morning, every clock running and no hour meaning what it used to mean. They have gotten good at it. There are lamps for it and pills for it and an entire company that sells the light that keeps you upright when your body has forgotten how to lie down. The economy has never been stronger. The obituaries have never been longer.

They say the last good night's sleep in Vísperas was eleven years ago, and that a child was born at dawn the next morning who has never once closed his eyes since.

That part, at least, is true.

I know, because I married him.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and getting ahead of yourself is the great sin of a city that never sleeps. Let me go back to the beginning, to the last winter of the long waking, when I was sixteen and my brother was dying by inches and I still believed the worst thing in the world was to be the only person on my street who could still close her eyes.

## Part One: La Vigilia

### Chapter One: The Girl Who Still Dreams

Alba woke, and that alone made her a criminal.

Not by law. There was no statute against sleeping in Vísperas — you could not outlaw a thing nobody could do anymore, the way you could not outlaw flight. But there were fewer than she could count on her hands, in the whole tenement, in the whole crooked hillside district of Vela Vieja, who still went under at night and came back up in the morning, and every one of them had learned to keep it quiet. Sleep had become the rarest thing in the city, and rare things got taken.

So she lay still for a moment with her eyes shut, the way she always did, and she counted. The particular ache behind her ribs that meant she had dreamed. The grit in the corners of her eyes. The strange soft feeling of having been somewhere and come back. She hoarded it. Then she opened her eyes to the gray four-in-the-morning light that in Vísperas was the same as the light at noon and the same as the light at midnight, because the sky over the city had not been properly dark in eleven years — there was always the wash of it, the great tidal glow of a million windows and the white beacon at the top of the Needle turning, turning, turning.

She had dreamed of her mother again. She held onto it the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing already it was leaving. Her mother at the kitchen table, cutting a mango into the shape of a hedgehog the way she used to for Teo when he was small, the blade going *tock tock tock* against the board, her mother looking up and starting to say something —

Gone. Alba pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw sparks, but you could not chase a dream backward. That was the first rule of dreaming, and the cruelest. You only ever got to keep the leaving.

Two years her mother had been gone. And still the only place Alba could find her was down in the dark of sleep, three or four nights a week if she was lucky, in fragments — a table, a blade, a mango, the beginning of a sentence. It was the reason she had never once been tempted by the light. Everyone told her she should take the light. The light would let her stay up clean and clear like a normal person, the light would stop her being strange and hunted, the light would fix her. But the light was the thing that had stopped everyone else from dreaming, and if she took the light she would lose the table, the blade, the mango, the beginning of the sentence. She would lose the only door left to her mother.

She would rather be hunted.

She had learned young how to hide it. By nine she knew not to yawn in the street — a yawn was a confession, in Vísperas, a thing that made hollow-eyed strangers turn and stare with an expression she had no name for until she was older and understood it was hunger. She knew not to mention dreams, not to describe the strange countries behind her eyes, not to say *I slept badly* the way other cities' children could, because there was no bad sleep here, there was only the impossible fact of any sleep at all. She had trained herself to come up out of the dark each morning without moving, without gasping, without any of the tells, so that even the neighbors through the thin tenement walls would not guess that the Cruz girl on the third floor still had the thing they had all lost. She wore her dreaming the way another child in another city might have worn a birthmark it had been taught to keep covered — a private strangeness, a thing that set her apart and could not be shown.

The city outside the window did not know it was sick. That was the part that had always frightened her most. A person with a fever knows to lie down; Vísperas had simply rebuilt itself around the fever until the fever looked like health. There were night-schools and dawn-markets and a whole industry of the small comforts the sleepless needed — eye drops, the good bitter coffee, the lozenges that numbed the particular ache of a body that has forgotten how to stop. There was a channel that played all night, every night, cheerful and loud, because a mind left alone in the small hours starts to drift, and drift, in Vísperas, was the first step of a road that ended in the fog. Everyone she knew had someone on that road. Everyone she knew was on it a little themselves. And over all of it, always, turned the white eye of the Needle, so constant that Alba had stopped seeing it the way you stop seeing your own nose — a fixture, a fact, the one light that never blinked, watching the city it would not let sleep.

She pulled the thin blanket up over her knees and let herself have, for one more moment, the private strangeness. The grit in her eyes. The ache behind her ribs. The leaving-taste of her mother. Then she folded it all away where no one could see it, the way she folded everything, and got up to face another morning that would look exactly like the middle of the night.

"You dreamed." Rita's voice came from the doorway, dry as paper. Alba's grandmother did not sleep — nobody Rita's age did anymore, the old were the first the waking took — but she had a way of sitting through the nights in the front room with her rosary and her memory that was as close to rest as anyone in the house got. "I can see it on you. You've got the look. Like a cat that got into somewhere it shouldn't."

"I always have the look."

"You always dream." Rita said it the way another grandmother might say *you always leave the light on,* fond and worried in one breath. "Come. Your brother had a bad hour."

The cold that went through Alba then had nothing to do with the winter.

Teo was eleven. He had been born the same spring the city stopped sleeping, which meant he had never in his life had one proper night, and for a long time that had seemed almost normal — half the children in his class were the same, the *nacidos despiertos,* the ones born awake, twitchy bright-eyed kids who ran themselves ragged because their bodies did not know how to stop. But something had changed in Teo over the autumn. He had gone quiet. He had started forgetting the ends of his own sentences. And two weeks ago, Alba had come into his room and found him standing at the window with his eyes wide open, staring at the turning light on the Needle, not answering to his name — and when she'd finally shaken him out of it he'd looked at her and said, in a voice that was not quite his own, *there's a lady in the water and she can't wake up,* and then he'd blinked and asked what was for breakfast as if nothing had happened.

That was the second stage. Everyone in Vísperas knew the stages now the way an older generation had known the phases of the moon. First *el desvelo,* the plain sleeplessness that took you in a week or a year. Then *la niebla,* the fog, when the edges of things began to run and time started skipping like a scratched record and you saw people in doorways who weren't there. Teo was in the fog. And after the fog came the last stage, the one people did not say out loud, the one that had taken Alba's mother — when a person got up one ordinary hour and walked out the door and into the streets and simply did not come back, because some part of them had wandered off into a place the rest of them couldn't follow, and the body kept walking a while longer out of habit, hollow, until it lay down somewhere and went still.

They called those ones *los velados.* The veiled. There were more of them every month. You saw them on the boulevards at dawn, walking slow with their faces turned up to the light, and you learned to cross the street.

Alba was not going to let that happen to her brother. She had promised herself, over her mother's empty bed two years ago, that it would not happen twice.

She went to him now. Teo was sitting up, small and hot, his hair stuck to his forehead, his eyes too big. "I was somewhere," he said. "I was somewhere and I couldn't find the way out and it was your fault, and then it wasn't your fault, and then —" He stopped. His mouth kept moving for a second without sound, the way the fog made you do, like a radio between stations. Then he found himself again. "Alba. My head hurts."

"I know, corazón." She sat on the edge of the bed and gathered him in, and he was so light, he had gotten so light. "Close your eyes. I'll give you an hour."

"You'll get in trouble."

"Let me worry about trouble."

He knew what she was offering, and he was too tired to argue. He turned his face against her shoulder. And Alba did the thing she had never told a doctor about, the thing she was fairly sure would have gotten her taken away years ago if anyone official had seen it — she closed her own eyes, and she let herself go down toward sleep, that first warm tilting slide, and she held her brother tight against her, and she pushed.

It was not something anyone had taught her. It had just always been there, the way some people can wiggle their ears. When she went under while holding on to a person, she could take them partway with her — could pour a little of her own sleep into them like water from one cup to another. It didn't last. An hour, maybe two. But for that hour, whoever she held would sleep, really sleep, the deep clean kind that the whole city had lost, and wake a little less broken than before.

She felt Teo's breathing change against her, felt the awful bright tension go out of his small body all at once, felt him drop into the dark like a stone into a well. She held the door open for him with everything she had.

And in the drifting place just below waking, the place she went when she did this — the place that was not quite a dream and not quite the room — she heard, very far off, the sound of water. And a voice in the water, faint, patient, unbearably tired, that she was almost sure had said her name.

She surfaced with a gasp. Teo slept on, peaceful, in her arms.

Rita was watching from the doorway, and her old face had gone the color of ash.

"That sound," Rita said. "The water. You heard it."

Alba stared at her. "How do you —"

"Because I heard it too, mija. Fifty years ago." Rita crossed herself, slow, the way she did when a thing was serious. "It means she's stopped being able to wait. Get dressed. There are things I should have told you a long time ago, and we are almost out of time to tell them."

### Chapter Two: The Boy Born Awake

But Rita would not tell her that morning after all. Because that morning there was a black car parked at the bottom of the hill.

Alba saw it from the stairwell window and knew it for what it was the way you know a wasp in a room. It was too clean for Vela Vieja, where the cars were all held together with faith and wire; it was the deep glossy black of the company, and on its flank, small and silver and tasteful, was the mark she had been taught to fear before she was taught the alphabet: a single upright flame inside a circle. The mark of Vela S.A. The company that lit the sleepless city. The company that, everyone in the district knew and no one could ever prove, came quietly in glossy black cars to collect people who could still do things with sleep that ordinary people couldn't.

"They're not for us," Rita said, but her hands were not steady. "They can't be for us."

"I'm not waiting to find out." Alba was already thinking. She could not stay in the flat, and she could not lead them to Teo, and she needed the one thing she did when the walls closed in, which was to move, to be out in the crowd, to disappear into the city that never emptied and never slept and where a girl could always vanish. "Keep Teo down. Keep the light off. I'll go out the back and lead them off if I have to. I'll be at the water market by seven."

"Alba —"

"Tell me tonight. All of it. I promise I'll come back to hear it."

She went down the back stairs three at a time and out into Vísperas at four in the morning, which looked exactly like Vísperas at any other hour: awake.

This was the thing outsiders — when there had still been outsiders, before the ships stopped coming — could never get used to. In a normal city the small hours emptied the streets. In Vísperas there were no small hours. The bakeries were open and the barbers were open and the schools ran three shifts because children who never slept could not be kept in a bed for eight hours they had no use for. The buses ran. The markets roared. Old men played dominoes on the corners at three in the morning with the fixed, glassy concentration of people who had forgotten there was any other time to be doing it. Everywhere, on every surface, was the light — the Vela light, the warm white glow of the lamps the company sold and the strips it laid along every avenue, the light that kept a sleepless body upright and a sleepless mind from noticing how far it had drifted. It hummed. If you stood still, which nobody did, you could hear the whole city humming, a single low electric note that never stopped, day or night, day or night, day.

Alba moved through it with her hood up and her head down, a small dreamer in an ocean of the awake, and she did not notice the boy following her until he was walking beside her.

"They came for you, didn't they," he said. "The flame car."

She jerked away. He put both hands up at once, easy, unhurried — a tall boy about her own age, seventeen maybe, with the worst case of the eyes she had ever seen. Everyone in Vísperas had the eyes now, the sleepless eyes, red-rimmed and too wide. But his were something else. His were the eyes of someone who had been awake not for weeks or years but for his entire life, who had never once in seventeen years drawn the curtain, and there was a terrible clarity in them, a thinness, as if he were seeing her and something just behind her at the same time.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"You do." He fell into step with her again, careful to stay an arm's length off, reading her the way you'd read a nervous animal. "I've seen you before. You give the old ones on Calle Sol an hour of sleep sometimes. Sunday nights. You think nobody sees because everyone's looking at their phones, but I see everything. I see everything all the time. That's sort of my whole problem." A crooked, exhausted smile. "You're a dreamer. A real one. And Vela hunts real ones, and there's a flame car at the bottom of your hill. So."

Alba stopped. In the roar of the market street, in the hum of the light, she looked at him properly for the first time. "How do you know what I do?"

"Because I never sleep," he said, simply, "and it turns out if you never sleep — never, not once, not for your whole life — you start to see the parts of the city that only show up around the edges of it. The parts that live in the place between awake and asleep." He said it without drama, the way you'd mention a food allergy. "I was born the morning the city stopped. My mother used to say I came out with my eyes open and I've kept them that way out of spite. Damián." He didn't offer his hand; in Vísperas nobody touched, touch was how the fog was rumored to spread, though that was probably nonsense. "And right now there is a woman in a gray coat two hundred meters behind you who has been on you since Calle Sol, and she has the flame on her collar, and you should turn left at the fish stall and keep turning left until I tell you to stop."

Alba turned left at the fish stall.

She did not know, yet, whether to trust him. But she had lived her whole life in a city that had taught her one iron rule — the awake could not be read, they all looked the same, hollow-eyed and humming — and this boy had read the gray coat off a stranger's collar at two hundred meters in a crowd of ten thousand. Whatever he was, he saw things she couldn't. And she was being hunted, and being hunted was a lonely business, and here at least was someone who had noticed.

They turned left, and left, and left, through the back of the fish market where the ice ran pink into the gutters, and Damián talked the whole time in a low steady voice, telling her where the gray coat was without ever looking back, and Alba realized she was following the directions of a boy she'd met ninety seconds ago into the oldest and darkest part of Vela Vieja, and that she felt, for the first time in two years, almost safe.

He moved through the crowd like water finding the cracks in stone — never against the flow, always into the gap that was about to open, a half-second before it opened. She understood, watching him, that this was what seventeen years of never sleeping did to a person if it didn't kill them: it made them see the seams in everything. Where the awake saw a solid wall of bodies, Damián saw the one path through; where they saw four in the morning, he saw the exact quality of a particular streetlight that told him the gray coat had turned back. It should have made him powerful. Instead it had plainly made him tired in a way that went all the way to the bone, a tiredness she recognized because she carried its opposite — she was the girl who could rest and hid it; he was the boy who never could and couldn't hide it at all, it was written in every red-rimmed line of him. Two halves of the same broken thing, she thought, and did not know yet how right she was.

"Left again," he murmured, and she went left, and trusted him, and hated a little how easy the trusting was.

"Here," Damián said finally, pulling her into the mouth of an alley so old and narrow the buildings leaned together overhead and blotted out the beacon-light for the first time all night. In the sudden dimness his pale eyes seemed to hold their own faint glow. "She's lost you. She'll circle back to the hill." He let out a breath. "You've got maybe an hour before she reports in and they send more."

"Why are you helping me?"

Damián was quiet for a moment. When he answered, the easy patter had gone out of his voice, and what was underneath it was so raw she had to look away.

"Because you can do the one thing I have wanted my whole life," he said. "You can sleep. And I've spent seventeen years watching a city full of people who lost it and mourn it and would sell their own children to get it back — and I never had it to lose in the first place. Never once. Not for a single hour. So when I saw a girl who could give it away for free, to strangers, on Calle Sol, on a Sunday —" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I wanted to know what kind of person does that. That's all. I wanted to know."

Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, a black car slid to a stop, and the doors opened, and the humming of the whole city seemed to catch and hold its breath.

### Chapter Three: What Rita Knew

They did not catch her that night.

That was Damián's doing — Damián who knew the between-edges of the city, who pulled her sideways through a laundry and out through a coal cellar and up a set of stairs that Alba would have sworn were not there when they started climbing them, until the black car and the gray coat were somewhere far below and behind, and they came out gasping onto a rooftop in the gray dawn light with all of Vísperas spread beneath them, blazing, sleepless, humming, going about its endless business under the turning white eye of the Needle.

And there, from the roof, in the honest light of a morning that felt like a mercy even though it looked like every other hour, Damián told her the first true thing about the city she had lived in her whole life, and Alba told him hers, and by the time the sun was properly up they had traded enough of themselves that they were no longer strangers, which in Vísperas, where nobody had the patience for anything anymore, was practically a marriage.

She told him about Calle Sol, because he'd already seen it and there was no use pretending. About don Emilio, who had been a ship's carpenter before the ships stopped coming and the sleep stopped with them, and who had held on through eleven years of the waking on nothing but stubbornness and the good bitter coffee until the fog started taking the edges of him — the way he'd begun calling Alba by his dead wife's name, the way he'd started setting two cups. How she had found him one Sunday sitting on the step outside his building at four in the morning, weeping without knowing he was weeping, and she had sat down beside him and taken his hand, an old man's hand, all knots and history, and gone under, and given him an hour. How he had slept against her shoulder like a child, and woken clear, clearer than he'd been in a year, and looked at her and said her right name and thanked her and been ashamed. How she went back every Sunday after that, and then it was don Emilio and the widow Paz from the second floor, and then it was six of them, then nine, the oldest and the most far-gone on her street, a little congregation of the fading that met in the small hours to be given, one at a time, the hour of rest the whole city had lost.

"It doesn't fix them," she said. She was looking at the city, not at him; it was easier. "An hour a week against a whole life of no sleep — it's nothing, really. A cup of water thrown on a house fire. But don Emilio says it's the difference between drowning and drowning *slower,* and that slower is worth more than I know, when you're the one drowning. So I keep going back." She shrugged, a small tight motion. "I can't take the light and I can't fix the city and I couldn't save my mother. Giving an old man an hour is the one useful thing I've ever found that I can actually do. So I do it. Every Sunday. Even though it's stupid. Even though it's dangerous. Even though — " she gestured, vaguely, at the whole hunted morning — "even though it's how they found me."

Damián was quiet for a while. Then he told her his.

He told her about his mother, who had died when he was twelve — not of the fog, of an ordinary thing, a fast bad illness, the kind that had always killed people and always would. He told her how she used to sit up with him the whole night every night, because a baby who won't sleep needs someone awake beside him, and a child who never sleeps needs it more, and a boy who never once in his life has closed his eyes needs it most of all, and she had done it, alone, for twelve years, worn down to nothing, so that he would never spend a single one of his endless nights afraid and by himself. He told her she used to make up cities for him to walk through in the dark, since he couldn't dream them — she'd describe streets, whole streets, out loud, hour after hour, a city of the mind for a boy who would never get one behind his own eyes. And how, after she died, he had gone out and found that the city she'd described *existed* — that it was the Duermevela, the drowsing city, the one only he could see — and he had never been able to decide whether she'd known about it somehow, or whether she'd simply loved him so well that she'd built him, out of pure invention in the dark, the exact map of the only country he'd ever get to call his own.

"So I walk it," he said. "Every night. Alone. Have done since I was twelve. Seven years." His crooked, exhausted smile had gone somewhere very sad. "You want to know the truth of why I followed you? It wasn't only that you could sleep. It's that you were the first person I ever saw *give it away.* Everybody in this city hoards what little rest they can scrape together — they'd step over their own grandmother for an hour of it. And there you were on Calle Sol handing it out for free to old men who'd forgotten your name. And I thought — " He stopped. Started again, rougher. "I thought, my mother would have liked her. That's the whole thought. That's all it was. *My mother would have liked her.* And I've been alone in that city in the dark for seven years and I wanted, just once, to walk it with someone my mother would have liked."

The sun came up gray-gold over the sleepless city while he said it, and neither of them moved, and Alba understood that they had told each other the true things — the mother-shaped holes they each walked around, the useless brave habits they'd built to keep from falling in — and that this, the trading of the true things, was a kind of touching that Vísperas had almost forgotten and that no light could keep you awake through. When she finally reached over and took his hand again it was not to be led anywhere. It was just to hold it. He let her. Neither of them said anything about it. In a city where touch was rumored to spread the fog, it was the most reckless thing either of them had done, and it did not feel reckless at all. It felt like the first sane thing in eleven years.

Then she went home to hear the rest from Rita.

Her grandmother was waiting at the kitchen table with two cups of coffee gone cold and a photograph Alba had never seen. It was old — cracked white at the corners, the colors gone to rust and honey — and it showed a girl of maybe seventeen standing in front of a stone cistern, laughing, her hand resting on the shoulder of another girl who was asleep. Actually asleep, in the daytime, in the open, her head tipped back against the stone and her face slack and peaceful in a way no face in Vísperas had looked in eleven years.

"That's me," Rita said, tapping the laughing girl. "And that —" her finger moved to the sleeper, and hovered, and did not quite touch it — "that is her. La Durmiente. The one the old story is about. She was real, mija. She is real. And I knew her."

Alba sat down slowly.

"When I was a girl," Rita said, "the city still remembered. Not everyone. But enough. There were families — old families, of no importance, cleaners and night-watchmen and the sort of people nobody photographs — who kept the bargain. We were called *serenos.* You'd know the word as the old name for a night watchman, the man who walked the streets calling the hours and telling you all was well. That's what we were, but the thing we watched over wasn't the streets. It was her. La Durmiente slept beneath the city, in the old cistern under the cathedral quarter, and she dreamed the city's nightmares so the city wouldn't have to, and the serenos took turns sitting with her. Keeping her safe. Keeping her fed — she ate lullabies, if you can believe such a thing, songs and quiet and the small kindnesses of strangers. And keeping her *asleep,* because the one thing she must never do, we were taught, the one catastrophe, was wake."

"Because if she woke —"

"Because if she woke, all the nightmares she was holding would have nowhere to go but back into the city that made them. Eleven years of them. Fifty. A thousand. All at once." Rita's hands closed around her cold cup. "We kept the bargain for centuries, mija. Quietly. Badly paid and never thanked and mostly forgotten."

Rita turned the cracked photograph in her hands, and for a moment the dry voice softened into something Alba had never heard in it — the voice of a girl of seventeen, fifty years gone.

"I sat with her twice a week," she said. "My mother had done it, and her mother, back and back. You went down into the cistern under the cathedral quarter — cold stone, the smell of old water, one lamp — and there she was on the low bed we kept for her, asleep, always asleep, that face I showed you, so peaceful it broke your heart. And your job was simple. You kept her company. You kept the lamp trimmed. You sang to her — soft old songs, cradle songs, the sort of thing you'd sing to a baby, because that was what she ate, mija, I told you and you didn't believe me: kindness, quiet, the small good things people do when they think no one is watching. A sereno's real work was to be, for a few hours, the proof that the city she carried was worth carrying. And when the nightmares came up through her — because you could see them, faint, moving under her eyelids, the city's whole night pouring into her — you'd take her hand and you'd say the words. *Estoy aquí. No estás sola. Todo está bien.* I'm here. You're not alone. All is well. That was all. That was the whole of it. You couldn't take the dark off her; no one could; but you could make sure she never carried it in the dark *alone.*"

"Did she talk to you?" Alba asked. "Did she know you were there?"

"Sometimes. Not in words, mostly. But — yes. She knew." Rita's thumb moved over the sleeping face in the photograph. "Once, when I was about your age, I'd had a terrible week — my father was dying, the slow bad way — and I went down to sit with her and I couldn't manage the songs, I just put my head down on the edge of the bed and cried. And in her sleep she moved her hand. Found my hair. Rested it there. A girl who was carrying the nightmares of the whole city, and she reached out in the middle of it to comfort *me.*" The old woman's eyes were bright. "That's who they buried, mija. That's who they turned into a streetlight. Not a monster. Not a machine. A girl who, drowning under everyone else's dark, still had it in her to reach for a crying stranger's hair. I have thought about that hand every day for fifty years."

She fell quiet a moment. Then the dryness came back into her voice, and with it a new hardness, the sound of an old woman arriving at the part of the story she was ashamed of.

"And then we stopped. My generation. The last of us." She would not look at Alba now. "It got harder to believe, you understand. The world got loud and bright and modern, and going down into a cold cistern to sing to a sleeping girl our grandmothers swore held up the sky — it started to feel like superstition. Like something you'd be laughed at for. The young ones stopped coming. I stopped coming. God forgive me, I told myself she'd be fine, she'd slept a thousand years, what was one lazy generation. We let the watch lapse. We forgot her on purpose because remembering her asked too much of us." Her hands closed hard around the photograph. "So when the company dug her up and found no one guarding her, no serenos, no one to say *stop, you don't know what she is* — that was our doing too, mija. Vela stole her. But we're the ones who left the door unlocked. And then the company came."

She said *the company* the way another woman might say *the sickness.*

"Vela. They were nothing, at first — a lamp company. Then someone at Vela dug in the wrong place. They were putting in the foundations for that —" Rita jerked her chin toward the window, toward the impossible silver spire that dominated every view in the city, the Needle, la Aguja, Vela's tower — "and they broke into the old cistern, and they found her. And instead of leaving her be, instead of coming to the serenos who could have told them what she was —" Rita's voice cracked, and she took a moment, and when she went on it was very quiet. "They saw a resource. A woman who made sleep. In a city they wanted to keep awake, so it would keep buying their light. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They didn't want to help her sleep. They wanted to *keep her from it.* Because a city that can't sleep on its own will pay anything, anything at all, for the light that lets it stay upright. She was bad for business, asleep. She was worth everything, awake and suffering, with her held dreams draining out of her a little at a time to be bottled and sold as their miracle light."

Alba thought of the Needle. Of the white beacon at its peak that turned and turned and never went out, that you could see from any window in the city, that she had looked at every single night of her life without ever once wondering what it was.

"They took her up," she whispered. "Didn't they. Up to the top. Into the light."

"Eleven years ago." Rita nodded, and a tear ran down into the deep lines of her face. "They took her out of the dark where she belonged and they carried her to the top of that needle and they put her in the light, mija, the light that never goes off, and they have kept her awake up there ever since. And down here we've been dreaming her nightmares for her, all of us, all the time, awake — because she can't hold them anymore, and someone has to, and now it's everyone. That's the sleeplessness. That's *la Vigilia.* It's not a sickness. It's her nightmares, loose in the streets, looking for a place to land." Rita reached across the table and took Alba's hands, and her grip was fierce. "And your mother knew. Your mother was the last sereno who tried to reach her. Two years ago. That's where she went, mija, the morning she walked out and didn't come back. She didn't wander off in the fog like the others. She went to the Needle. She went to try and free the one who sleeps for all of us."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the city hummed.

"Then I'm going too," Alba said.

### Chapter Four: The Needle

You did not simply walk into the Needle.

Alba learned this the hard way over the three days that followed, three days of watching the tower from every angle Vela Vieja offered, three days of Damián shaking his head at every plan she floated. The Needle rose eight hundred meters out of the reclaimed heart of the old cathedral quarter, a single tapering blade of silver and light, and everything about it was designed to say *not for you.* The lower two hundred floors were offices, the machinery of a company that lit an entire coastline. Above that, the public could go no further. And above *that,* in the long bright taper that ended in the turning beacon, there was nothing on any map, no floor numbers, no lifts that the directory admitted to — just the place where, if Rita was right, an ancient woman had been kept screaming-awake in a cage of light for eleven years while her stolen dreams poured out over the city below.

"You can't go up the inside," Damián said. They were on their rooftop — it had become theirs, in three days, the way things did when you had no time — passing a paper cup of the bitter black coffee that was the only thing that tasted of anything anymore. "Every floor is badged. Every corridor is watched. They'd have you before the second landing."

"Then how did my mother get in?"

"She didn't get *in.*" Damián was quiet. He had a way of going quiet before he said the hard things, as if he had to walk out onto them carefully to test they'd hold his weight. "Alba. There's a thing I haven't told you, because I didn't know how, and because once I tell you you're going to want to do it and it's going to be dangerous in a way I can't protect you from." He turned the cup in his hands. "The city has two versions. There's the one everybody lives in — the awake one, the humming one, the streets and the light. And there's the other one, laid right on top of it, the same streets seen through half-shut eyes. The drowse. I've walked in it my whole life because I've never been anything *but* half-shut — I'm awake enough to stand up and dreaming enough to see it, all the time, and it's exhausting and it's the loneliest thing in the world and it means I can go places nobody else can go. They call it *el Duermevela.*"

Alba knew the word. It was an old, soft, ordinary word — the name for that state right at the lip of sleep, the drowse, the half-doze. Her mother had used it. *No estoy dormida, mija. Estoy en el duermevela.* I'm not asleep. I'm just drowsing.

"The Needle in the waking city is a fortress," Damián went on. "But in the Duermevela it's — different. Everything's different there. The rules are the rules of nearly-sleep. Distances lie. Locked doors open if you don't look at them straight. And the one place in the whole city where the two versions press hardest against each other, where the drowse is *thickest* —" he pointed, up, at the turning beacon — "is right there. At the top. Because that's where she is. She's leaking. Eleven years of the deepest sleeper who ever lived, kept awake, and it's worn the wall between the two cities down to nothing right at the point of that needle." He set the cup down. "You can't climb the Needle awake, Alba. But you can walk up it in the Duermevela. If you can get in. And the only person who can get into the Duermevela is a dreamer — a real one — going under on purpose, right at the base of the tower, with someone beside her who knows the way."

"Someone like you."

"Someone like me," he agreed, and neither of them said the other thing, the thing that had been building on the rooftop for three days in every shared cup and every not-quite-touch: that a boy who had wanted to sleep his whole life and had never managed it was about to walk a girl into the one place in the world made entirely of the sleep he'd been denied. That he would be the guide to a country he could see but never enter. That he was doing it anyway, for her, and that they both knew it.

"There's a catch," Alba said. "There's always a catch. What's the catch."

"The catch," said Damián, "is that in the Duermevela, the nightmares are loose. All of them. Everything she used to hold, everything the city's dreamed awake for eleven years, it's all *there,* wandering. And they're drawn to dreamers. You'll be the brightest thing in that whole dark city, Alba. A real sleeper walking through a country of nightmares. They'll come for you like moths." He finally looked at her, and his pale eyes in the beacon-light were frightened and steady at the same time. "I can't stop them. I want to be honest about that. I can show you the way and I can see what's coming before you can and I can shout, but I can't fight your dreams for you. Nobody can do that but you." A breath. "So. Still going?"

And there was the other clock, the one that made the three days of scouting feel like a cruelty. Teo was getting worse. Alba had watched it happen in fast-forward over those three days, the way you watch weather come in off the sea — the forgetting spreading from the ends of his sentences to the middles, then to whole afternoons; the standing at the window; the two mornings she had woken from her own stolen sleep to find him already up, already at the glass, already turned toward the turning light, and had had to say his name three times, then five, then take his face in her hands before he came back. She had started giving him an hour every night instead of once a week, pouring more of herself into him than was safe, and it bought less each time. The fog did not care how much you loved someone. It came in anyway, patient, tidal, and every hour she spent watching the Needle from a rooftop was an hour her brother slipped further out.

"He asked me this morning where Mamá was," she told Damián, on the third night, on the roof, not looking at him. "Not — not like he forgot she was gone. Like he could see her. Like she was in the next room and he was cross with me for keeping him from her." Her voice did not shake, which was worse than if it had. "That's the last stage. That's where they go right before they walk out the door. My mother saw people who weren't there for about a week, and then one morning she just — got up, and went. To the Needle, it turns out. To do the thing I'm about to try to do." She finally looked at him. "So we don't have three more days to be clever, Damián. I don't think we have one. I think if I don't go up that tower tonight, the next time Teo gets up and walks to the window, he's not going to stop at the glass."

Damián didn't tell her she was wrong. That was one of the things she was already coming to love about him, though she would not have used the word yet — that he never told you comfortable lies to be kind. He just looked at the Needle for a long moment, and then at her, and said, "Then tonight. While they're still turning the district over looking for you down here, and not up there." And that was when, below them, at the base of the hill, so far off it was almost gentle, came the sound of a car door. Then another. Alba looked down and saw them: not one flame car now but three, and figures spreading out from them into Vela Vieja, going door to door, and she understood with a cold lurch that they had run out of the luxury of being careful, that Vela had decided a dreamer who could give sleep away for free was worth turning the district over for, and that the next door but one was hers, was Rita's, was Teo's —

"They're at my building," she said.

"Then we're out of time to decide." Damián was already up, already moving, already holding out his hand — the first time in three days either of them had offered to touch — palm open in the turning light. "The base of the Needle. Now. While they're looking down here and not up there." His fingers curled. "Still going?"

Alba took his hand. It was the first hand she had held while wide awake in two years, and it was warm, and it was shaking, and it did not let go.

"Still going," she said.

### Chapter Five: The Drowse

They reached the base of the Needle as the shift-bells were ringing across the city — not that shifts meant anything, not that bells meant anything, but Vela loved a bell, Vela loved to pretend the day had a shape — and Damián led her off the boulevard into the shadow of the tower's great foot, into a service alcove between two of the enormous supports where the hum of the light was so loud it was almost a pressure on the skin.

"Here," he said. "This is as close as the waking city lets you get. Sit."

She sat, her back against cold silver metal that thrummed against her spine. The Needle went up and up and up above them until it vanished into its own brightness. Somewhere at the top of all that height was the Sleeper. Somewhere above that was the turning eye.

"Now the hard part," Damián said, crouching in front of her. "You have to go under. Really under, the way you do for the old ones on Calle Sol — but this time you don't hold on to a person, you hold on to *me,* and instead of pouring your sleep into someone, you take yourself down into it and step sideways. Into the drowse. I'll be right beside you. I'm always in it; I never leave it; I'll be the one thing in there that's steady, I promise you. Follow my voice. Don't look at anything straight on. And whatever you see —" his hand tightened on hers — "it isn't real yet. Not until you believe it. That's the only rule that matters up there. Nightmares are only as real as you let them be. Do you understand?"

"No," Alba said honestly.

"Good. Nobody who understands it would go." A ghost of the crooked smile. "Close your eyes, Alba."

She closed her eyes.

And because she had done this ten thousand times, because sleep was the one thing her body knew how to do that the whole rest of the city had forgotten, she went down easily — that first warm tilting slide, the well opening under her, the hum of the light fading, her mother somewhere in the dark of it with a mango and a blade — down, and down, and then Damián's voice, low and close: *not all the way, Alba, stop here, right here, at the drowse, and open your eyes* — and she opened her eyes in a different city.

It was Vísperas, and it was not.

The same streets ran away in the same directions, but the light was gone — not dark, exactly, but dim, the specific dimness of a room seen through lashes, everything soft-edged and slow. She knew this place. That was the vertigo of it. She had walked these exact streets ten thousand times awake, and here they all were, faithful down to the crack in the curb outside the bakery and the crooked lamppost on the corner of Calle Sol — but rendered in the grammar of nearly-sleep, the colors gone to the muted washed tones of a memory, the edges of the buildings not quite meeting the sky, the whole city holding still in the way a room holds still when you're almost under and haven't yet fallen. It was Vísperas the way you'd see it if you lay down in the middle of it and let your eyes drift half-shut and looked at your own life through your lashes: the real city, softened, slowed, and somehow — she felt this before she could have said why — *sadder,* the way a house is sadder at the edge of sleep than at noon, heavy with all the things that only surface when you finally stop moving fast enough to outrun them.

The great hum was gone too, and in its place was a silence so total it rang. And the city was not empty. Figures moved through the dim streets, slow and wandering, and Alba understood after a moment that she was seeing them — *los velados,* the veiled, the lost ones, the people whose bodies wandered Vísperas at dawn with their faces turned up; here in the Duermevela were the parts of them that had wandered off, drifting through the drowsing streets, looking for a door home they would never find.

She recognized one of them. That was the thing that nearly undid her before they'd taken ten steps — a woman drifting past the mouth of an alley, slow, dreamy, her face turned up the way they all turned their faces up, and Alba knew her, it was the widow Paz from the second floor, one of her Sunday congregation, who had stopped coming three weeks ago and whom everyone had quietly assumed had gone into the fog. Here she was. Or here was the part of her that had wandered off, still in her housecoat, still wearing the little gold cross she never took off, drifting through the drowsing city looking for a door. Alba started toward her without deciding to.

"Don't." Damián's hand closed on her arm, gentle and absolute. "You can't bring her back. Not from here — this is only the piece of her that already left; the rest of her is lying in a bed somewhere with a hollow in it where she used to be. If you go to her, if you touch her, you'll just stop where she stopped. That's how it takes you. Not with claws. With someone you love, standing in a doorway, so glad to see you." His voice was very kind and very tired. "You'll see a lot of them. People you know. The city's been feeding this place for eleven years. The only way through it is to keep your eyes soft and keep walking and let them drift. I'm sorry. I know how that sounds. But it's the only rule that's ever kept me alive in here."

Alba made herself stop. Made herself look away from the widow Paz's upturned face. It was the hardest thing she had done since her mother's empty bed, and she understood, with a cold lurch, that it was going to get harder, that this whole city was going to be a corridor of people she loved held out like bait, and that Damián had walked it alone every night for seven years.

"How do you stand it," she whispered. "Seeing them. All the ones you knew."

"You don't stand it," he said. "You just keep walking, and you promise yourself that if you ever find a way to actually bring them home, you'll come back for every single one." He drew her on, into the drowse, away from the widow's drifting shape. "That's the promise. That's what keeps your feet moving. Now come on. Eyes soft. Don't look at anything straight on."

And there were the others. The things that were not people. She saw them at the edges, the way Damián had told her to — never straight on — great slow shapes moving between the buildings, made of the stuff of bad dreams, a childhood terror the size of a bus, a grief with too many arms, the particular nightmare of a whole city that had not slept in eleven years, all of it loose, all of it wandering, all of it beginning, as she stood there shining like the only lit window in a dead street, to turn toward her.

"They see me," she breathed.

"They see you." Damián was beside her, and in the Duermevela he looked *more* solid, not less, as if this were the only place he had ever fully belonged. He took her hand again. Above them the Needle rose into the drowsing dark, and near its distant point a light still burned — one light, the only light left in this whole dim city, faint and terrible and sorrowing. "That's her. That's where we're going. Straight up, all the way to the top." He pulled her into the first street, away from the turning shapes, into the silence, into the drowse. "Walk fast. Don't look at anything straight on. And Alba —" as the first of the wandering nightmares fell in behind them, slow and patient and certain — "whatever you dreamed of, whatever you've been going under two years to see — you're going to meet it up there. She's got everyone's. Including yours. Including your mother's. Are you ready for that?"

Alba looked up the long dim spire toward the one grieving light.

"No," she said, and walked toward it anyway.

## Part Two: El Duermevela

### Chapter Six: The City Through Lashes

The first thing Alba learned about the drowsing city was that it did not want to be walked in a straight line.

She would fix her eyes on the Needle — there, ahead, the one grieving light at the top of everything — and set off toward it down a street she knew, Calle Sol, the street where she gave the old ones their hour, and three turns later the street would have delivered her somewhere else entirely, a plaza she'd never seen, a stairway that went down when it should have gone up, a door standing open in the middle of the air. In the waking city, distance was a thing you could trust. Here it was a mood. The Duermevela measured the way between two places not in meters but in *feeling,* and the feeling of wanting to reach the Sleeper at the top of the Needle was, it turned out, very far indeed.

"You're trying too hard," Damián said. He walked the drowsing streets the way she walked the waking ones — loose, easy, unbothered — and she hated him a little for it, and understood that this was the only place in existence where he got to feel that way, and forgave him. "You can't want it directly. Nothing here comes to you if you reach for it. You have to go sideways. Think about something else and let the tower catch up."

"That's insane."

"It's the drowse. Have you never chased a name you'd forgotten? The harder you grab, the further it goes. Then you give up and think about your shoes and it drops right into your head." He touched her elbow, steered her gently off the straight path and onto a curving one that seemed, maddeningly, to lead away from the Needle. "Tell me about your mother instead. The mango thing. You started to tell me on the roof and then you stopped."

So she told him about the mango, cut into the shape of a hedgehog, the diamond scores in the flesh so you could turn the skin inside out and the cubes stood up in rows like little spines, and how Teo had shrieked with laughing the first time, and how her mother had done it every Sunday after, even when they were poor, *especially* when they were poor, because it cost nothing and it made a feast out of a single piece of fruit, and how Alba had not been able to look at a mango since. And while she talked the curving street straightened without her noticing, and the Needle was closer than it had been, and she understood the trick of the place: you got where you were going by being somewhere fully, and there was no being fully anywhere, in the drowse, except inside a memory.

That was the danger of it too. She felt it the moment she understood the rule — how easy it would be, in a country made of memory, to walk into a good one and never come out. She thought of *los velados,* the wandering lost ones drifting through the dim streets around her, and understood at last what had happened to them. They had found a memory warm enough to stop in. That was all. They had not been dragged anywhere. They had simply stopped, in some Sunday kitchen of the mind, and let the waking city walk on without them.

"That's what took the ones out there," she said. "They're not lost. They're *home.* They found a good enough dream and they stayed."

Damián nodded slowly. "Now you understand why Vela's light works. It's not really keeping people awake. It's keeping them out of *here.* Out where the good dreams are. Because if the whole city ever remembered how good it is in the drowse —" He stopped. "Nobody would ever go back to work again."

They walked. The nightmares kept their distance, for now — great slow shapes at the ends of streets, wheeling, patient, aware of her, unhurried in the way of things that know their quarry cannot leave the neighborhood. Damián watched them the way a sailor watches weather. And Alba began, cautiously, to hope, because they were climbing now, the streets tilting up toward the base of the tower, and the one grieving light at the top was no longer a pinprick but a lamp, and she could feel — faint, far, patient, unbearably tired — the pull of the thing that had said her name in the water.

And then one of the great slow shapes stopped keeping its distance.

It came around a corner ahead of them — not fast, nothing in the Duermevela was fast, but certain, the way a tide is certain — a nightmare the size of a house, made of some childhood terror grown monstrous with eleven years of feeding, all wrong angles and a smell like a flooded cellar and a low sound underneath it that Alba felt in her teeth before she heard it in her ears. Every instinct she had screamed to run. She turned to run.

"No." Damián caught her, hard, and put himself between her and it, and did not look at it. "Running tells it it's real. Running is how you vote. Alba, listen to me — look at it, but don't *believe* it. It's the size of a house because someone, somewhere, half-asleep on a platform, is giving it that size right now. It only weighs what you agree it weighs." His grip was iron and his voice was steady and his pale eyes were fixed on hers, not on the thing bearing down on them. "You give the old ones an hour of sleep by pouring yours into them. You can pour the opposite into this. You can pour *doubt.* Look at it and find the seam — every nightmare has one, the place where it stops making sense — and put your attention right there, and don't flinch, and it comes apart. Try. I'm right here. It cannot have you unless you hand yourself over."

Alba made herself turn. Made herself look at the thing — the wrong angles, the cellar-smell, the sound in her teeth. And because she had spent her whole life inside dreams, because she knew the texture of them the way a weaver knows cloth, she found it: the seam, the place where the terror stopped being true. It was too much. That was the seam. It was trying too hard. A real fear is specific and small and shaped exactly like the thing you love losing; this was a bully's cartoon of fear, a pile of loudness with nothing underneath, and the instant she saw that — saw the frightened half-asleep stranger on a platform somewhere flinching a house-sized shadow into being out of nothing — it stopped being frightening and became, unbearably, *sad.*

"You're not even real," she said to it, wonderingly. "You're just someone very tired, being scared of the dark."

The thing came apart. Not with a scream — with a sort of sigh, a great slow unmaking, the wrong angles softening, the cellar-smell thinning to nothing, the sound in her teeth guttering out, until there was only a drowsing street and the faint after-image of someone's fear, dissolving, harmless, gone. Somewhere in the waking city, Alba knew without knowing how, a stranger on a platform had just stopped shaking, and blinked, and wondered why they suddenly felt lighter.

Damián let out a long breath. "Nobody's ever done that on their first night either," he said, and there was something new in how he looked at her, something careful and amazed. "It takes people years to learn to unmake one. Most never do; they just run their whole lives." He shook his head slowly. "You're going to be dangerous up there, Alba. In the good way. Come on. But save your strength — that's one. There's a whole city of them, and the closer we get to her, the worse they get."

They walked on, and Alba felt it, the new steadiness in her own chest, the knowledge that the dark could be unmade if you refused to vote for it — and she was so busy holding on to that small hard-won courage that she almost walked straight into the crowd before she saw it.

Then the drowsing street ahead of them filled with people, and every one of them was asleep on their feet, and every one of them turned to look at her at once.

"Don't stop," Damián said, very low. "Don't look at any single one. Keep walking. We're coming to the Market. It's the worst part before the tower and there's no way around it. Whatever it offers you — and it's going to offer you things, Alba, it's going to offer you *her* — you keep walking. Promise me."

But Alba had already seen, at the far edge of the crowd of sleepers, at the mouth of the Market, a woman cutting a mango into the shape of a hedgehog, *tock tock tock,* and looking up, and starting to say something.

### Chapter Seven: The Market of Small Sleeps

The Market of Small Sleeps was where the city came, without knowing it, to dream.

Alba understood this the moment they entered it, though no one had explained it and no sign announced it. It filled the great drowsing plaza that in the waking world was the transit hub, the place ten thousand sleepless commuters passed through every hour — and here in the Duermevela some drowsing part of every one of them lingered, browsing the stalls, and the stalls sold dreams. Not big ones. Small ones. A stall where you could buy back the smell of your grandmother's house. A stall of first kisses. A long low table where a vendor with no face laid out, on velvet, the specific afternoon light of a day you had been happy and forgotten you were happy. The sleepers moved among them slow and rapt, and their real bodies, Alba knew, were somewhere up top in the humming waking city, standing on a platform, staring at nothing, while some deep part of them shopped down here for the rest they weren't allowed to have.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated that it was true.

"It's bait." Damián's hand was firm on her arm, steering, steering. "Every one of these is a place to stop. That's all a nightmare is, in the end — a dream you can't leave. Even the sweet ones. *Especially* the sweet ones. Keep walking. The far arch. Don't —"

And then the Market found *him.*

Alba felt his hand go slack on her arm and turned and saw a stall she had somehow not seen a moment ago — a low quiet stall, no vendor, just a bed. An ordinary narrow bed with a white pillow and a turned-down sheet, and above it, in no language and every language, the offer that the Market had reached seventeen years to find: *rest. real rest. lie down, just once, and know what everyone else has known. we've been keeping it for you.* And Damián — who had walked this city seven years and never once been tempted, who knew every trick the drowse could turn, who had just told her to keep her eyes soft and keep walking — Damián had stopped, and was looking at the bed with a hunger so raw and so total that Alba's heart cracked straight across.

"Damián," she said.

"I could just —" His voice had gone very young. "Alba, I've never. Not once. My whole life. And it's *right there.* One time. Just to know."

"It's not real." She stepped in front of him, into his eyeline, the way he'd taught her, the way she gave the old ones their hour. "Damián, look at me. It's the Market. It's the exact shape of your wanting and there's nothing under it — it doesn't know how to give you sleep, it only knows how much you want it. You told me that yourself, an hour ago, about my mother. *The end of the sentence isn't something you can be handed at a stall.*" She took his face in both her hands, and his terrible clear eyes were wet. "You want to know what real sleep is? Then get me up that tower, and free the girl who makes it, and heal the whole city — and I will give it to you myself, the true kind, with my own hands, the way I give it to the old ones on Calle Sol. I promise you. Not a stall's lie. The real thing. But you have to *walk past this,* Damián. You have to want the true one more than the fake one. That's the whole trick. It's the only trick there is."

For a long, terrible moment he looked past her at the bed, at the white pillow, at seventeen years of denied and dreamless nights. Then he shut his eyes — Damián, who never shut his eyes — and let her turn him, and walked past the offer of the one thing he had wanted his whole life, holding on to her instead. It cost him something; she felt it cost him, felt him shaking. It was, she thought, the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do, and she had not yet seen him walk a whole city home.

They were almost to the far arch when the last stall reached for her.

"Alba." Her mother looked up from the mango, and smiled, and it was her, it was exactly her, down to the small scar on her thumb from a childhood accident with the same kind of knife. "Mija. You're so tall. Come sit. I made your favorite."

Alba stopped walking.

She could not have done otherwise. Two years. Two years of going under three nights a week to catch a table, a blade, the beginning of a sentence — and here was the whole thing, the whole kitchen, the whole woman, the sentence with its end attached, and her mother was patting the stool beside her and there were two rows of little mango spines standing up in a bowl, and the light in the stall was the light of a Sunday, and Alba's whole body leaned toward it the way a plant leans at a window.

"You found me," her mother said. "I knew you would. You always were the one who could find me. Sit down, mija. You've been walking so long. You've been so tired for so long. Sit down and I'll cut the fruit and you don't ever have to walk anywhere again."

"Alba." Damián's voice, from very far away. "Alba, it's the Market. It's not her. Your mother went to the Needle. Your mother's *up there.* This is the thing that stops people. Alba, look at me —"

But it was her mother's hands, and her mother's scar, and her mother's voice saying the thing Alba had wanted to hear for two years, which was *you don't ever have to walk anywhere again,* and oh, she was tired, she was so tired, she had been tired since she was fourteen years old standing over an empty bed, and here was a stool and a bowl of fruit and the end of all the walking —

"What was the end of the sentence," Alba heard herself say.

Her mother paused, the knife lifted. "What?"

"In my dreams. You always start to say something and I always wake up before the end." Alba's eyes were streaming and she did not remember starting to cry. "So tell me. If you're her. What were you always going to say?"

And the thing wearing her mother's face opened its mouth, and nothing came out, because it did not know, because it had never been the woman who cut the fruit, only the shape of the wanting, only the bait — and in the half-second of its silence Alba understood that the real end of the sentence was not something you could be handed at a stall. It was something you had to go and get.

"You don't know," she said. "She'd know."

She turned, and took Damián's hand, and walked out of the Market of Small Sleeps while behind her the thing with her mother's face called her name in her mother's voice, over and over, until the sound of it thinned into the ringing silence and was gone.

Damián didn't say anything until they were through the far arch. Then, quietly: "Nobody's ever done that. Walked out. I've watched hundreds go in. I've never once seen anyone walk out."

"I'm not staying in a kitchen," Alba said. Her voice shook. "Not while the real one's at the top of that tower. Not while my brother's dying in the real one." She wiped her face hard with the back of her wrist. "The end of the sentence is up there. Everything real is up there. Let's go get it."

### Chapter Eight: What the Light Is Made Of

Past the Market, the drowsing streets ran out.

There was simply nothing more of the city — the buildings thinned, then guttered, then stopped, the way a dream frays at its edges, and ahead lay the great drowsing foot of the Needle, rising out of the last of Vísperas into a dark that was not the sky but the plain absence of anything dreamed. And built up the flank of the tower, spiraling into that dark, was a stair.

"That's new," Damián said, and for the first time all night he sounded uneasy. "That wasn't here before. I've been to the foot of the tower a hundred times and there was never a way up. The Duermevela made a stair." He tilted his head, reading it the way he read everything. "Or she did. She knows we're coming. She's making a road."

They climbed.

It was the strangest climb of Alba's life, because the stair did not behave like a stair. Sometimes it was a grand thing of pale stone with a rail; sometimes it was the back stairs of her own tenement; once, for a dozen steps, it was the aisle of a bus, and she climbed between the seats while sleeping commuters swayed and did not wake. The drowse showed you the climb you could bear. When she was frightened it gave her the back stairs of home, familiar and worn; when she was steady it gave her grand pale stone, as if to say *yes, this is worthy of you, keep going.* Once, on a stretch where her courage failed her badly, it simply gave her Damián's hand and nothing else — no steps at all that she could see, only his fingers laced through hers and the certainty in his voice saying *there's a stair here, I promise, put your foot down* — and she put her foot down on nothing and it held, because he'd said it would, and she understood that this was the truest thing the drowsing city had shown her yet: that when you cannot find the ground yourself, sometimes the only stair there is, is someone you trust telling you to take the step. She took it. It held. They climbed. And all the way up, the light at the top grew — not brighter, but nearer, and clearer, and Alba began to see what it was made of.

It was made of dreams. Vela's light, the light that lit the sleepless city, the warm white glow that hummed in every street and lamp — it was the Sleeper's stolen dreaming, drained out of her at the top of the tower and pumped down through the whole structure to be sold. Alba could see it now that she was inside the Duermevela, inside the truth of things: a vast slow siphon, threads of soft light drawn out of the grieving lamp at the peak and braided down and down through the Needle's core, out into the grid, into every bulb in Vísperas. Eleven years of it. They had taken the deepest sleeper who ever lived, the woman who dreamed a whole city's nightmares so it could rest, and they had turned her, drop by drop, into streetlights.

They stopped to rest on a landing that was, for the moment, the stone stair of some grand old building neither of them had ever seen. The climb had no end that Alba could find; every time she looked up, the grieving light was nearer and the top was no closer, in the maddening arithmetic of the drowse. Her legs did not ache — you did not ache, exactly, in a dream — but something deeper than her legs was tiring, some cord that ran between her sleeping body far below and the self that climbed here, and she understood she could not stay in the Duermevela forever, that there was a clock on this even if she couldn't read its face.

Damián sat close beside her on the step. In the waking city they had held hands twice, recklessly; here, where the rules were the rules of nearly-sleep, the small distance between them seemed to hum, charged, the way the air hums before rain. She was aware of him the way you are aware of a lamp in a dark room — the warmth of him, the particular quality of his attention, those terrible clear eyes that had never once looked away from anything in seventeen years and were, right now, looking at her.

"You keep watching me," she said. "In here. You did it in the Market too."

"Because in here you *dream,*" he said simply. "You go a little bright at the edges when you're deep in the drowse — everyone does, everyone who can sleep — you glow, faintly, like the last window lit on a dark street. I've been walking this city seven years and I never once saw it up close, because I never had anyone to walk it with, and everyone else who's ever in here is either lost or a nightmare." His voice was low. "I told you I followed you because you could do the thing I can't. That was true and it wasn't the whole of it. The whole of it is that watching you sleep is the closest I will ever get to knowing what it's like. You're the only country I'll ever visit." A crooked, unbearable smile. "So yes. I keep watching you. I'm sorry if it's strange. I'm cataloguing a place I can never go."

Alba looked at him — the loneliest boy in the world, glowing faintly himself now in the drained gold light, closer than he'd ever been — and something in her chest went over like a wave finally reaching the beach. She could have closed the small humming distance. She wanted to; she felt the want in her own hands. And she understood, at the same time, that this was the exact danger Damián had been warning her about all night in a different shape — that here, at the lip of sleep, wanting to *stop,* wanting to stay in a warm moment forever, was how the drowse took you. That if she kissed him now, on this endless stair, they might simply never climb the rest of it. There was a girl at the top of the tower who had been awake for a thousand years, and a brother on a hill falling into the fog, and the whole drowning city, and the not-yet of it all sat between her and Damián like a promise held in trust.

"When this is over," she said, and did not finish, because she didn't have to; his eyes changed, and he nodded, once, and the not-yet became a thing they were both holding, carefully, together, up the whole rest of the climb.

"When this is over," he agreed, rough. "Come on. Up. The sooner it's over —" He got to his feet and pulled her to hers, and did not let go of her hand, and this time neither of them pretended it was only to lead the way.

"That's obscene," she whispered — meaning the light, the siphon, the girl turned drop by drop into streetlamps, dragging her attention back to the thing that mattered.

"That's business." A new voice — a woman's, warm, cultured, amused — came down the stair from above, and it was in the waking world and the drowse at once, doubled, ringing. "Though I'll grant you it looks worse than it is when you put it like that. Come up, Alba. You've come so far. Let me at least give you the tour before you decide to hate me."

Damián went rigid. "That's Vela. That's Renata Vela. She's *here* — she's in the Duermevela — Alba, she shouldn't be able to *be* here, nobody but a dreamer —"

"Oh, I'm no dreamer," said the voice, closer now, and around the curve of the impossible stair came a woman of perhaps fifty, silver-haired, beautifully dressed, entirely solid, entirely calm, standing in the drained light of the tower she had built. "I haven't dreamed in eleven years. Longer. I made very sure of that." Renata Vela smiled, and it was a kind smile, a tired smile, the smile of a woman who had done a terrible thing for what she believed were excellent reasons and had stopped needing anyone to agree. "But I own the light this whole place is made of, child. And when you own the light, you can go wherever it shines." She gestured up, toward the grieving lamp, toward the top of everything. "Including all the way to the top. Where you're going. Where your mother went." The smile did not waver. "Would you like to see what happened to your mother, Alba? I can show you. It's the least I owe you, before I decide what to do with you. Come up. She's been waiting a long time for someone to come and finish what she started."

### Chapter Nine: Renata

Renata Vela climbed ahead of them, unhurried, talking, and Alba followed because the truth was up the stair and there was no way to the truth that did not go past this woman.

"I know about your brother," Renata said, almost gently, three steps up. "Teo. Eleven years old, second stage, deteriorating fast — my people have had a file on your whole family since your mother came up here, Alba. I know he stands at the window now. I know he's asked where your mother is. I know you have days, maybe hours." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse; she said it like a doctor, which was what she had been. "I can stop it. Tonight. I have a wing of this building full of the only people in Vísperas who understand what the fog actually is, and I have more of the light than God, and if I choose to, I can pull your brother back from the edge and keep him there. Clear-headed. Himself. For years." She paused on the stair and looked back, and her eyes were kind and absolutely transactional. "All you have to do is stay. You're a natural dreamer — a real one, the strongest I've seen in eleven years, strong enough to walk all the way up here in the drowse, which no one but that boy has ever done. She's failing, up top. I've known it for months; I can only drain a mind so far before it gives out, and hers is nearly out. I need a new source. I've *needed* one for months. And here you are, walked right up to my door, worth more than the whole grid — a dreamer who could light this city for another eleven years." The offer hung in the drained gold light, obscene and reasonable at once. "Take her place. Willingly, comfortably, well cared for — nothing like what I did to that poor creature up top, I've learned, I can be humane about it. Take her place, and I will save your brother, and light your city, and you will have done more good than any one girl in history. That's my offer. Think about it while we climb. It's a long way up, and I find people make better decisions when they've had the whole climb to be honest with themselves about what they're actually willing to trade."

Alba said nothing. But she felt the offer do exactly what it was built to do — felt it find the softest, most frightened part of her, the part that had stood over her mother's empty bed and promised *not twice, never twice* — and she understood that the hardest thing on this whole tower was not going to be the nightmares, or the climb, or the machine at the top. It was going to be this. A cure for Teo, held out in a kind woman's hand, with only a girl's whole life as the price. The oldest bargain in the city, offered fresh, to her.

"You think I'm a monster," Renata said, not turning around. "That's fine. It's efficient, thinking that. But I'd like you to understand the shape of it, because you're clearly going to make some grand gesture up top and I'd rather you made it with your eyes open." She trailed a hand along the rail, and where she touched it the drained light brightened, obedient. "Do you know what I did, before Vela? I ran a clinic. Sleep disorders. Insomnia, night terrors, the whole miserable catalog. I was good at it. I believed in it — I believed that a person who couldn't sleep was a person in pain, and that the pain could be treated, and that rest was a human right." A small dry laugh. "I had a daughter. Marisol. She had terrible dreams. From the time she was small — night terrors, the kind where they sit up screaming with their eyes open and don't know you. I treated everyone else's children and I couldn't fix my own. And one night when she was fifteen she had a dream she couldn't wake up from." The hand on the rail stilled. "She just — didn't come back. The body slept on for a while. The doctors had no name for it. This was before the Vigilia; this was the first case, though nobody knew it then. My daughter was patient zero for a city that would forget how to wake up, and I sat by her bed for eight days and watched her dream herself to death, and there was not one single thing I could do, because you cannot follow anyone into a dream. Nobody can. That's the whole horror of it. We die in there alone."

Alba said nothing. Beside her Damián had gone very quiet.

"So no," Renata said, resuming the climb. "I don't think sleep is a human right anymore. I think sleep is where they take the people we love and we are not allowed to follow. I think the dark is a door that only opens one way. And when I found *her* —" she gestured up — "when my engineers broke into that cistern and I climbed down and saw a woman who had been asleep for a thousand years and was *still breathing,* still dreaming, unkillable, drowning in other people's nightmares for a *thousand years* — do you know what I felt? Not greed. Everyone thinks it was greed." She stopped on the stair and finally turned, and her eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "I felt *vindicated.* Here was proof. Here was the enemy with a face. The thing that had taken my daughter, the appetite under the city, the sleep that eats people — and for once, for the first time, I could *reach* it. I could put it in the light. I could keep it awake, the way it had kept my Marisol asleep, and I could make sure it never, ever took another child into the dark again." Her voice cracked, just once, and steadied. "The light was mercy, Alba. I lit the whole city so no one else's daughter would ever be taken through that door. That people pay me for it is beside the point. I would have done it for free."

"You didn't stop the door," Alba said quietly. "You just moved everyone to the wrong side of it. Now nobody sleeps. Now everybody's dying awake instead of asleep. My brother's eleven, Renata. He's in the fog. He's going to walk out one morning and not come back, and it won't be a dream that takes him — it'll be *your light.* It'll be eleven years of her nightmares with nowhere to go because you won't let her hold them." She was crying again and she didn't care. "You lost one daughter to sleep. You're losing the whole city to no-sleep. It's the same grief, Renata. You just spread it out so thin you can't see it's the same shape."

Renata Vela looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the certainty — not agreement, not yet, but the first hairline crack in a thing that had been sealed for eleven years.

"You sound like your mother," she said at last. "She said something very like that. Right before she went up." She turned, and resumed the climb, and her voice when it came again was flat and tired and afraid. "Come and see, then. Come and see what happens to people who think they can free her. It's just up here. It's the last thing your mother ever saw, and I've kept it exactly as it was, because I am not, whatever you think, a woman who looks away from what she's done."

They climbed the last turn of the stair, into the full grieving light, and Alba saw what was at the top of the Needle.

### Chapter Ten: The One Who Sleeps for All

She was enormous, and she was a girl.

Both were true at once, in the way things were true in the Duermevela. If Alba looked at her straight on she was vast — a sleeping figure the size of the tower's whole crown, filling the great glass chamber at the peak, her breathing the slow tidal thing Alba had felt as a pull from the streets far below. But if Alba looked at her sidelong, the way Damián had taught her, she was only a girl of seventeen, thin, dark-haired, laid on a table of steel in a cage of a hundred lamps, her wrists loosely bound, her chest rising and falling too fast, her eyes moving frantic and desperate under their lids — because they would not let her open them, and they would not let her close them into true sleep either. They kept her exactly at the lip. In the drowse. In the duermevela, forever, the state that is neither rest nor waking, the state that is only suffering. Eleven years.

And around her, thick as smoke, was everything she held. Alba could see them now, pressed into the glass chamber, straining — the nightmares, the city's whole eleven-year backlog, every terror that should have belonged to a butcher or a banker or a frightened child, all of it dammed up behind this one girl's failing sleep, held in only by the last thin thread of dreaming they had not yet managed to drain out of her. She was the wall. She had always been the wall. And the wall was almost gone.

"*Ayúdame,*" said the Sleeper, without moving her lips, in the voice from the water, in a voice like the whole city sighing. *Help me.* "*Estoy tan cansada.*" I'm so tired. "*Déjame dormir. O déjame despertar. Cualquiera. Ya no puedo con el medio.*" Let me sleep, or let me wake. Either one. I can't bear the middle anymore.

Alba came closer, and the closer she came the more she saw of what had been done here, and the worse it was. It was not a shrine and it was not even a prison, exactly. It was a *machine.* Around the steel table the hundred lamps were mounted on articulated arms, angled with terrible precision at the girl's face, at her eyes, calibrated — Alba understood, sickened — to hold her at the exact pitch of the drowse, never dark enough to sleep, never bright enough to wake, the cruelest possible setting, maintained by engineers with clipboards for eleven years. And from the crown of her head ran the thing Alba had followed up the whole tower: the siphon, a slender bright cord drawn out of the very top of her dreaming and threaded down into the machinery of the Needle, where it split and split again into the ten thousand strands that lit the city. They were not just keeping her awake. They were milking her. Every soft white bulb in Vísperas, every lamp Alba had grown up under, every strip of light along every avenue — it had all come out of this girl, one bright drop at a time, drained from the top of a mind held forever at the edge of a rest it was never allowed to reach.

There was a small brass plate bolted to the base of the table. Alba bent to read it, and wished she hadn't. It was an inventory tag. It gave the installation a number, and a commissioning date eleven years past, and a single line of description, the way you'd label a boiler or a transformer: *PRIMARY LUMINANCE SOURCE — DO NOT INTERRUPT.* Someone had reduced the oldest sleeper in the world, the girl who had reached out of a thousand years of nightmares to comfort a crying child, to four words on a maintenance tag. *Do not interrupt.* Alba stood up shaking with a rage so pure and so cold it frightened her.

"Eleven years," she said aloud, to the machine, to the tag, to the whole silver tower. "You put her here for eleven years and you wrote *do not interrupt.*"

"*No llores por mí,*" the Sleeper murmured, though Alba had not known she was crying. Don't cry for me. "*Han sido mil años. Once no es nada.*" It's been a thousand years. Eleven is nothing. And that — that it was *true,* that to the girl on the table eleven years of this was merely the most recent and best-documented stretch of a thousand-year sentence — was somehow the thing that finally broke Alba's heart all the way open.

Alba's mother was there too.

She saw her the moment she stopped looking for her — the trick of the place, always the trick of the place. Not at a stall, not a bait, not a shape of wanting. Her actual mother, or what was left of her: a faint figure standing at the foot of the steel table with her hand resting on the Sleeper's ankle, translucent, worn nearly to nothing, one of the *velados,* the lost — but she had not stopped in a warm kitchen like the others. She had climbed all the way up here two years ago and reached the Sleeper and then simply run out of self, and stayed, standing guard over the suffering girl with the last of what she was, because that was what a sereno did.

"Mamá," Alba whispered.

The figure turned. And this time, when her mother started to say something, Alba did not wake up, and the Market was not there to fake it, and there was nothing between them but the grieving light.

"The end of the sentence," her mother said, faint as breath, smiling, finishing at last the thing she had been trying to say across two years of broken dreams, "was *don't come after me.*" A tired, loving, exasperated mother's smile, the last one, the real one. "And here you are anyway. *Terca. Igual que yo.*" Stubborn. Just like me. "You can't free her by taking her place, mija. That's what I got wrong. That's what I came up here to do — climb onto the table, be the wall, let her rest, one sereno for one Sleeper, the old bargain — and it doesn't work. One person isn't enough to hold what a whole city dreams. It never was. She's not a woman doing a job, Alba. She's a *debt.* She's every nightmare the city refused to carry, all pushed onto one back, for a thousand years." The figure was fading even as it spoke, giving Alba the last of itself in words. "You can't pay a whole city's debt with one girl. Not her. Not you. Not me. The only way to free her —"

"Is to make everyone carry their own," Renata said, from the doorway, in a voice gone strange. She was staring at the girl on the table, at the frantic eyes, at the thin bound wrists, and something in her face was coming apart. "That's what she was going to say. Give the nightmares back. To everyone. All at once." Renata's certainty had cracked all the way through now, and behind it was pure terror. "Do you have any idea what that would do? Eleven years of held nightmares, released into a waking city, into every mind at once? It would be the worst night in the history of the world." She looked at Alba, and for the first time she looked old, and frightened, and unsure. "That's why I could never let her sleep. Not just for the light. If she sleeps, she stops holding them — and if she stops holding them before the city's ready to catch them, it all comes down at once and everyone drowns. I wasn't only keeping her awake to sell it, girl. I was keeping her awake because I was *afraid of the night she'd let go.*" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I have been holding my finger in this dam for eleven years and I am so tired, and I don't know how to take it out without killing everyone, and neither did your mother, and neither do you."

The Sleeper's frantic breathing hitched. One of her bound wrists strained. And high above the drowsing city, in the waking world, the turning beacon flickered — just once — for the first time in eleven years.

"She's slipping," Damián said. He had gone white. "Whatever we're going to do — she can't hold it much longer. She's letting go on her own. Alba." He gripped her hand. "The dam's coming down whether we're ready or not. So we'd better decide, right now, what kind of night this is going to be."

### Chapter Eleven: The Wall Comes Down

They had until dawn. Maybe less.

Alba stood in the grieving light between the woman who had built the tower and the boy who had never slept and the girl who could not stop, and she made herself think, fast and clear, the way she thought when Teo had a bad hour and there was no one else in the flat to do the deciding.

The old bargain was cruelty. She saw that now with her mother's last words still ringing. One person, holding a whole city's nightmares, forever — it was slavery, was all it was, a thousand years of one back bent under everyone else's dark, and it did not matter that the city had once called it holy. It had never been holy. It had been *convenient.* A whole city getting to sleep clean every night because one girl agreed, or was made to agree, or was too tired to keep refusing, to carry what should have been shared. Renata had not broken the bargain. Renata had just found a way to profit from it while making it infinitely worse.

And she had offered Alba a seat in it. That was the thing Alba made herself look at now, clearly, while the beacon flickered and the Sleeper's breathing hitched — the offer that had been sitting in her chest the whole long climb like a stone she couldn't put down. *Take her place, and I will save your brother.* It would work. Alba did not doubt for a second that it would work; Renata Vela was many things but she was not, Alba thought, a liar about medicine. She could walk down this tower tonight knowing Teo was safe. She could keep the promise she'd made over her mother's empty bed — *not twice, never twice* — and all it would cost was the rest of her own life on a steel table under a hundred lamps, a girl turned into streetlights, a new brass tag with a new number and the same four words. *Do not interrupt.*

And that, she understood, was exactly how they had always done it. Not with chains. With love. With a frightened girl's love for someone she couldn't bear to lose, held out over an altar, so that saying *yes* felt like devotion and saying *no* felt like letting a child die. A thousand years of Sleepers, and Alba would have bet everything that not one of them had been dragged. They'd have been *asked,* each one, at the worst moment of their lives, *will you trade yourself to save the one you love* — and of course they'd said yes, of course, who wouldn't, and the city had called it holy and slept easy on it for a thousand years.

"No," Alba said aloud, to Renata, to the tower, to the whole long lineage of that question. "I'm not taking her place, and I'm not letting you save Teo your way, because your way is the same rotten trade dressed in a hospital gown. You don't get to fix my brother by making me the next thing you drain." She was surprised by how steady it came out, how sure. Refusing had felt, her whole life, like the dangerous thing. It turned out the dangerous thing had always been saying yes. "Everyone's been trying to answer the wrong question," Alba said slowly. "You" — to Renata — "asked how to keep her awake so the nightmares stay dammed. My mother asked how to take her place so she could rest. You both assumed one person has to hold it. That's the whole rotten root of it. One person holds the dark so everyone else doesn't have to." She looked at the girl on the table, the frantic eyes, the thin wrists. "But nightmares aren't a *substance,* are they. They're not water in a dam. They're the things we're each afraid of. Mine are mine. Teo's are Teo's. Yours" — to Renata — "have your daughter's face. She's been dreaming *all* of them, alone, because we all handed ours over and looked away. Of course it's crushing her. We gave one girl the fear of a million people and told her it was her job."

"You can't just hand them back," Renata said, but weakly now. "It would break people. A nightmare you've never learned to dream is a nightmare you don't know how to wake up from. That's what killed Marisol — she got one dream too big to wake from, *one.* You want to give the whole city its whole backlog in a single night?"

"No," Alba said. "I want to give everyone one night of their own real nightmares — and I want to give them a reason to wake up from them." She turned to Damián. "That's you. That's what you're for. You've walked the drowse your whole life without ever getting lost in it. You're the one person in this whole city who's proof you can stand at the lip of sleep and *not* fall in. That's not a curse. That's the thing we need. If the wall comes down and the city falls into its own nightmares all at once, someone has to be awake in the drowse to shout them home. To be the voice that says *this is a dream, wake up, come back, this way.* One sereno couldn't hold the whole dark. But one watchman, walking the streets of the Duermevela, calling the hours, telling everyone *all is well* —" her voice caught on it — "that might be enough to get a whole city through a single bad night."

Damián stared at her. "There's a city of nightmares out there, Alba. Millions of them. One voice —"

"Not one." Alba looked at Renata. "Two. You said you'd hold your finger in the dam for eleven years. Fine. Now hold the *door* instead. You own the light — you can go wherever it shines. So light the way. Not to keep people awake. To help them find their way *back.* Turn the whole grid from a thing that stops sleep into a thing that guides people home from it. A city of night-lights instead of a city of floodlights. You've been the jailer for eleven years, Renata. Be the lighthouse for one night."

The chamber was silent but for the Sleeper's ragged breath. Renata Vela looked at the girl on the table — really looked, sidelong, the way you had to — and saw, Alba thought, not the enemy that took her daughter, but a fifteen-year-old who had been screaming with her eyes open for a thousand years, and was still, even now, holding the whole world's dark off the people who had done it to her.

"She looks like Marisol," Renata whispered. "They all look like Marisol, in the end. Every child I've kept awake to save. I've been doing to a whole city what I couldn't bear to have done to my daughter." Her hand went to her mouth. "*Dios mío.* What have I built."

"A tower with a light on top," Alba said gently. "We can decide what the light is for. But we have to decide now."

Above them the beacon flickered again — twice, three times — and the Sleeper's bound wrist finally, softly, slipped its binding, and her hand opened, and eleven years of a city's held nightmares began, all at once, everywhere, from every window in Vísperas, to come down like rain.

"It's starting," Damián breathed. "Alba. It's starting. Whatever we're doing —"

"We're waking the city up," Alba said, "by letting it fall asleep." She took his hand, and held out her other to Renata, and after a moment, the woman who owned the light took it. "All of us. Right now. Before the dark lands. *Corran.*"

## Part Three: El Alba

### Chapter Twelve: The Longest Night Begins

They came down the tower faster than they had gone up, because the Duermevela, which had made them climb toward a thing they wanted, now flung them down toward a thing they feared, and fear was the fastest road there was.

The stair unspooled beneath Alba's feet — grand stone, then tenement steps, then the aisle of a bus, then no stair at all, just a long tilting fall through streets that reassembled around her boots the instant before each one struck — and all the while, above and behind and now around them, the rain came down. Not water. The dark. Eleven years of a city's held nightmares, released from the failing girl at the top of the Needle, sheeting down through both cities at once, the drowsing one and the waking one, looking for the minds that had made them.

Alba had thought she understood what was coming. She had not.

The first nightmare to land near her hit a drowsing street corner and *bloomed* — a childhood terror, someone's, a thing of too many teeth and a voice like a parent's gone wrong — and the drowsing figures nearby, the shopping-sleep commuters, the wandering *velados,* all turned toward it with the slow helpless fascination of the deeply tired, and Alba felt the awful gravity of it even at a distance, the pull to stop, to look, to be caught. In the waking city, she knew, this same instant was landing on some real person standing on some real platform — the fog going thick, the edges running, a fifteen-year-old's night terror opening up under a grown adult who had no idea how to wake from a dream they were having with their eyes open. Marisol's death, Alba understood now with a lurch. Renata's daughter. Multiplied by a million, all at once, tonight.

"It's too much," Renata gasped. She was keeping up better than a woman of fifty in good shoes had any right to; terror was a great equalizer. "Alba, I *told* you — there are two million people in this city and they're all about to —"

"Then we split up." Alba skidded to a halt at a drowsing crossroads she knew — in the waking world it was the junction of the boulevard and Calle Sol, the heart of everything, the place the whole city passed through. Here it was a still dim plaza with the dark coming down on it like snow. "Three of us, three jobs. Renata — the light. Get to the grid. You said you can go wherever the light shines, so *go,* everywhere at once if you have to, and turn it. Every lamp in Vísperas. Stop it keeping people awake and make it *call them home* — make it warm, make it low, make it the light you leave on for a child so they can find the way back from a bad dream. Can you do that?"

Renata stared at her. Around them the dark fell.

"Marisol used to leave her lamp on," she said, very quietly. "The little one. With the paper shade. She said the dark had a door in it and the lamp kept the door shut." Something settled in the older woman's face — grief, still, but grief with a task attached, which is the only kind anyone can survive. "I have spent eleven years turning that lamp into a weapon. Yes. I can turn it back." She looked up at the Needle, at her tower, at the flickering beacon. "I can turn it *all* back. God forgive me, it was always just a lamp." She caught Alba's arm, hard, once. "Your mother would be unbearably proud of you, and she would tell you not to do the thing I can see you deciding to do, and you're going to do it anyway, aren't you." And then she was gone — not run, *gone,* dissolved into the nearest thread of light and up it, out through the grid, everywhere the light shone, which was everywhere.

Which left Alba and Damián alone at the still center of a drowning city.

"That's her job," Damián said. "You said three jobs." His pale eyes were very steady on her, and very afraid, because he had already guessed, the way he guessed everything, ahead of the words. "What's mine, and what's yours, and why do I already hate the answer to the second one."

"Yours is the round," Alba said. "The watchman's round. You're the only person alive who's ever stood at the lip of sleep his whole life and never once fallen in. Every person out there is falling in tonight, all at once, and Renata's light will show them the door but somebody has to be *in here,* in the Duermevela, walking the streets, being the voice that says *this is a dream, wake up, come back, this way.* The old serenos called the hours so the city knew it wasn't alone in the dark. That's you. That's what you were born awake *for,* Damián. Not a curse. A watch." She was crying and grinning at once. "You wanted to know what kind of person gives sleep away for free on a Sunday. I want to know what kind of person spends seventeen years being the loneliest boy in the world and then spends one night making sure nobody else is. Go find out."

Damián's jaw worked. "And you?"

"I'm going to my brother," Alba said. "And then I'm going back up the tower. To her. Because Renata's light and your round will get the city through the night — but the wall's still coming down, Damián. She's still up there, still holding what's left, still dying by inches. The dam doesn't stop until *she* does. Somebody has to be with her when she finally gets to sleep. Somebody has to make sure that when she lets go of the last of it, there's a person holding her hand instead of a hundred lamps." She swallowed. "My mother climbed all the way up there to do that and ran out of self at the last step. I'm not going to run out. I've got two years of practice at going under. I'm the best sleeper in this whole city." The grin cracked. "I'm going to sleep her free, Damián. It's the one thing I know how to do."

He kissed her then. In the falling dark, at the center of the drowning city, a boy who had never once closed his eyes closed them, and kissed her, quick and fierce and terrified, and it was the first thing either of them had ever done that was only for themselves.

"That's not goodbye," he said against her mouth. "I refuse it as goodbye. I'll walk the whole city tonight and I'll call every hour and the last one I call will be *dawn,* and you'll be there for it. Say you'll be there for it."

"I'll be there for dawn," Alba said.

And they broke apart, and ran in opposite directions into the longest night the city of Vísperas had ever known — the boy toward the drowning streets to call the lost ones home, the girl toward a small flat on a hill where her brother was falling, right now, into the worst dream of his life.

### Chapter Thirteen: The Watchman's Round

There had been serenos in Vísperas for four hundred years, and every one of them had walked alone, and Damián — running through the drowsing streets while the dark came down like the end of the world — finally understood why.

You could not do this in company. There was no time. Each street he turned into held a dozen drowning minds, and each one needed the same three things, fast: to be seen, to be told it was a dream, and to be pointed at the light. He learned the rhythm of it in the first block and never stopped for the next six hours.

A man on the corner of a drowsing avenue, standing rigid, his real body somewhere on a real platform, his face slack with terror as a nightmare bloomed around him — a debt, a shame, a door in a childhood house he'd sworn never to open. Damián did not touch him; you couldn't wake a man by grabbing him, he'd learned that at nine years old. He got low, got into the man's eyeline, and said the words in the flat certain voice of someone who had never for one instant been fooled by the drowse: "This is a dream. You're asleep on the platform. It can't follow you out. The light's behind you — see it? Warm one. Walk to it. That's the way home." And the man's eyes found the low warm lamp Renata had lit at the mouth of the street — she was doing it, Damián realized with a surge of something like joy, she was turning the whole grid, everywhere he went the killing floodlights were guttering down into soft gold homing-lights — and the man walked toward it, and woke, somewhere, on a platform, gasping, alive.

One. Two million to go.

He lost count somewhere in the first hour, and stopped trying to keep it, because counting was a waking-city habit and this was not the waking city. A girl of maybe nine, frozen in a drowsing schoolyard, trapped in the endless test-dream where the questions were in a language she'd never learned and the clock ran backward — he got low and told her it was a dream and there was no test and the warm light was the door, and she woke, somewhere, in a bed, and he never saw her again and never would and that was fine, that was the job. A man reliving the day the ships stopped coming, standing on a drowsing dock watching the same gray horizon empty itself over and over — Damián stood beside him and said, *the horizon's not your fault, it never was, come inside, there's a light on,* and the man turned from the sea and woke. An old woman looking for a house that had been torn down forty years ago, going door to door in a street that no longer existed — he could not give her back the house, no one could, but he could say *it's alright to have loved it, come toward the light, the people who lived there with you are waiting on the other side of morning,* and she went. He did not save them from their nightmares. That was the thing he understood, somewhere in the second hour, that no sereno before him had ever been able to teach because no sereno before him had ever had two million people to teach it to at once: you could not take a person's dark away, and you should not want to, because the dark was theirs, it was made of what they'd loved and lost and feared, it was the shape of their whole life. All you could do — all anyone could ever do for anyone — was stand in it beside them for a moment, and point at the light, and promise that morning was real.

But it was not two million, Damián understood after the first hour, because he was not alone after all. The old serenos had walked alone because there had only ever been one at a time. Tonight there was a whole city of the newly-woken, and every single person he pulled out of a dream stood blinking in the soft new light and then — this was the thing, this was the thing that undid him — *turned to the next drowning person beside them and started saying the words.* Because they had just heard them. Because they had just been saved by them. Because it turns out the only thing a person needs, to help someone out of the dark, is to have just been helped out of it themselves.

The man on the corner woke, and turned, and got low in front of a weeping woman, and said, "It's a dream. Look — the light. That's the way home." And she woke, and turned to a boy. And he woke, and turned to an old man. And it went out from Damián in a widening ring across the whole drowsing city, the round he could never have walked alone, walked now by two million people passing the words hand to hand: *this is a dream, wake up, come back, this way.*

He climbed to a rooftop near dawn — his rooftop, hers and his — to catch his breath and see the shape of it, and what he saw stopped his heart. The city was lit from below by ten thousand soft gold lights, and everywhere, everywhere, people were waking. Not fleeing the night. *Coming through* it. He could see them in the drowsing streets and in the waking ones both, sitting up, holding each other, some of them weeping, all of them — for the first time in eleven years — having had a dream and *survived* it. Having gone into the dark and come back and found it was only the dark, and that you could come back, and that the people around you would help you.

"They're doing it themselves," he said aloud, to no one, to the turning beacon, to the girl somewhere up inside it. "Alba. They don't need one watchman. They just needed to be shown *once.* You knew that. You knew it before any of us." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle, because he was so tired, tired in a way he had never once been in seventeen years of never sleeping — and he realized what the tiredness was. It was the good kind. The kind that comes after. The kind that the whole city had lost and was, right now, one weeping rescued mind at a time, remembering how to feel.

Below him a clock somewhere tried to strike an hour and gave up, the way clocks did in Vísperas. And Damián, the boy born awake, the last sereno, cupped his hands to his mouth over the healing city and did the thing his kind had done for four hundred years, the thing he had been born at dawn eleven years ago to be able to do, and called the hour into the dark:

"*¡Las cinco de la mañana, y todo está bien!*" Five in the morning, and all is well. His voice broke and he called it anyway, louder. "*¡Todo está bien, Vísperas! ¡Vuelvan a casa! ¡Ya viene el alba!*" All is well. Come home. The dawn is coming.

And two million people, waking from the first real dreams of their lives, heard a boy's voice call the hour, and believed him.

### Chapter Fourteen: The Lighthouse

Renata Vela had built the grid, and so the grid knew her, and tonight she poured herself through it like water through the roots of a tree.

She was everywhere the light was, which was everywhere. She was in the great floodlights along the boulevards, turning them down, down, from their killing noon-white to a low gold; she was in the strip-lights of the transit hub and the harsh tubes of the three-shift schools and the signs and the screens and the ten thousand lamps of Vela Vieja, and one by one, faster and faster as she got the knack of it, she turned the city's light from a wall against sleep into a hand held out through it. Warm. Low. The light you leave on for a frightened child. The light Marisol had asked for, with the paper shade, that kept the door in the dark shut.

It should not have been possible, to re-tune an entire city's grid in a single night — it had taken Vela's engineers three years to build the killing array, floodlight by floodlight, permit by permit. But Renata was not working through engineers now; she was *in* the light, and the light knew its maker, and it turned out that unmaking a cruelty was faster than building one, the way a held breath lets go faster than it was drawn. Boulevard by boulevard, district by district, she brought Vísperas down out of its eleven-year noon. She dimmed the great white towers of the financial quarter that had blazed all night for no one. She softened the harsh tubes of the night-schools where children who could not sleep had been made to learn in shifts. She reached the hospitals last and was gentlest there, laying the low gold light over the wards like a blanket, and in ward after ward she saw, through her own lights, the impossible thing beginning — patients who had not slept in a decade going quiet, going still, going *down* — and she had to stop for a moment, everywhere and nowhere, and simply take it in: that she, who had spent eleven years making very sure no one in this city could rest, was about to watch the whole of it fall asleep by her own hand.

And as she worked, the dark came for her too. Of course it did. She had held her nightmare off for eleven years with the very light she was now dismantling, and as each floodlight guttered down, the thing she had been keeping at bay by never sleeping, never dreaming, never once letting the dark near her, rose up through the softening glow to meet her.

Marisol.

Not the bait. Not a Market shape. The real memory, the true one, the eight days at the bedside, her daughter's face going slack, the doctors with no name for it, the terrible slow *leaving.* It came at Renata out of the dimming light and she nearly — nearly — flooded the grid back to full white, the reflex of eleven years, *put it back, seal it out, never look at it.* Her hand, wherever her hand was, twitched toward the master switch.

And then she heard it, far below and everywhere at once, carried up through the very lights she was tending: a boy's voice, calling the hour — and under it, threaded through the whole city, two million rescued minds taking up the words and passing them hand to hand.

They were all doing the thing she had never been able to do. They were going into the dark, and looking at the thing that lived there, and coming back.

"I never let myself dream you," Renata said to the memory of her daughter, in the dimming light, everywhere and nowhere. "Not once, in eleven years. I told myself it was to protect the city. It was to protect *me.* Because if I dreamed you I'd have to watch you leave again, and I couldn't, I *couldn't* —" The floodlight she was inhabiting flickered. She let it dim. She made herself, for the first time since a hospital room eight years and eleven years ago, stand still in the dark and look at her daughter's face. "But everyone out there is watching the ones they lost leave again, tonight, all at once, and coming through it, because there's a light on and a voice calling and they're not alone. And I built the light. I *built the light.* And I never once used it for myself."

She looked at Marisol. And she did the thing.

She let the dream come. She let her daughter's face go slack and leave, the way it had, the way it always would, and she did not seal it out, and she did not put the floodlights back, and it hurt exactly as much as she had known for eleven years it would hurt — and then it was over, the way a dream is over, and Marisol was gone, and Renata was still there, in the low gold light of the city she had finally set right, grieving, awake, *alive.* Come through it. On the far side. Where she had refused to go for eleven years and where, it turned out, you did not die after all. You just grieved. And then you kept the light on for the next person.

"All is well," Renata Vela said, and wept, and turned the last floodlight down to gold, and became, at long last, exactly what she had accidentally built: not a jailer. A lighthouse. A light on in the dark of a whole city, so that everyone lost in it could find their way home.

Far above her, at the top of the Needle, in the grieving chamber where a girl had screamed with her eyes open for a thousand years, the beacon — the last floodlight, the killing eye, the one that had kept the Sleeper awake for eleven years — flickered, dimmed, and went, for the first time in living memory, warm.

And in that chamber, Alba, who had climbed all the way back up while the city healed below her, felt the light change on her face, and looked down at the girl on the steel table, and saw her stop straining for the first time.

"There," Alba whispered, taking the Sleeper's thin, freed hand. "There. The light's kind now. You can rest. I've got the rest of it. Let me have the rest of it."

### Chapter Fifteen: What Alba Kept

But first — before the tower, before the Sleeper, in the thick of the longest night — there had been Teo.

Alba had run to him first. She had come down out of the Duermevela and up the hill to the flat in her waking body, gasping, while the dark came down and Vela Vieja fell into its dreams around her, and she had found her grandmother in the doorway of Teo's room with the rosary wound so tight around her fist that the beads had left red marks, and Teo —

Teo was gone. Standing at the window, eyes open, unblinking, gone into the fog and past it, his lips moving on words with no sound, his small body swaying toward the glass and the beacon beyond it. The last stage. The one that had taken their mother. Right at the edge of walking out the door and not coming back.

"He won't answer," Rita said, her voice destroyed. "He hasn't answered in an hour. Mija, he's going, he's *going,* the way she went —"

"He's not going anywhere." Alba crossed the room. She did not grab him; you couldn't wake anyone by grabbing them, she knew that now, she'd learned it in the drowse from a boy who'd learned it at nine years old. She got down in front of him, into his eyeline, small and fierce, and she took his face in both her hands, and she did the thing she had done ten thousand times on Calle Sol and never once for something that mattered this much. She closed her own eyes, and she went down — that warm tilting slide — and she held on to her brother and she did not pour her sleep into him this time. This time she went *after* him. Down into his dream. The one place, everyone said, no one could follow anyone. The place Renata had lost Marisol to. The place that had taken their mother.

She followed her brother into it, because she was the best sleeper in the whole city, and because a sereno does not let go.

It was the thing you were never supposed to do. Every rule she knew said so. You could give someone an hour of your own sleep — pour it into them, let them rest in the shallows of your dreaming — but you did not go *down after* someone into the deep of their own dream, because a dream is the most private country there is, and the deep of it is where people drown, and the one iron law of the whole sleepless age was the one Renata had learned at her daughter's bedside: *you cannot follow anyone into a dream. We die in there alone.* Alba had believed it her whole life. She had believed it standing over her mother's empty bed.

She did not believe it anymore. She had spent one night watching two million people do the impossible thing — go into the dark together, hold each other's hands across it, call each other home — and she had understood, somewhere on the tower, that *alone* had never been a law of dreaming at all. It had only ever been a lie the city told to excuse itself for not trying. So she took her little brother's hot face in her hands, and she went down after him, past the shallows, past the drowse, all the way down into the deep of Teo's own dream, into the place no one was ever supposed to be able to reach — and she reached it, because loving someone enough to follow them into the dark turned out to be exactly the thing that let you.

His nightmare was a small one, which was the worst of it.

His nightmare was a small one, which was the worst of it. Not monsters. A kitchen. Their kitchen, on a Sunday, but wrong — cold, the light gone gray, the table set for four and no one there, and Teo, small and alone in the middle of it, calling for their mother and their sister and getting no answer, only the *tick* of a knife on a board in the next room that stopped whenever he ran toward it. He was eleven. His whole nightmare was that everyone had left and would not answer and he was alone in the cold kitchen forever. He had been having it, Alba understood, for weeks. It was the fog. It was what the fog was made of. It was the fear that the people you loved would go into a room and never come out, which was not, in Vísperas, an unreasonable thing for a child to be afraid of, because it had already happened to him once.

"Teo," Alba said, in the dream, in the cold gray kitchen. "Corazón. I'm here."

He turned. His face — his real face, not the fog-slack thing at the window — crumpled. "You went in the room," he sobbed. "You went in the room like Mamá and you didn't come out and I called and I called —"

"I know. I know, mi amor. And here's the thing I came to tell you." She knelt and gathered him up, so light, so small, and she held him the way she'd held him a thousand nights, and she said the words, the sereno's words, the only words that had ever worked. "This is a dream. It's a bad one, and it's yours, and it's real the way dreams are real, but it's a dream, and you're allowed to wake up from it. I'm not in the room, Teo. I'm right here. I followed you all the way in here to tell you: you're not alone in the dark. You never have to be alone in the dark again. There's a light on. Rita's got it on in the front room. Follow it up. I'll be right behind you the whole way. Wake up, corazón. Come home. *Ya viene el alba.*"

And her little brother, in the cold gray kitchen of his own fear, looked at his sister who had come into the room and *not* vanished, who had followed him into the one place no one could follow, and he believed her — and the kitchen warmed, and the light came up gold in the window, and Teo took a breath in the dream and let it out in the waking world, in the flat, in Alba's arms, and blinked, and said, in his own small ordinary voice, "Alba? My head doesn't hurt."

Rita made a sound like the whole eleven years coming out of her at once.

Alba held her brother and her grandmother in the low gold light — Renata's light, the kind light, come up warm even here in the poorest window on the hill — and for one moment let herself simply be a girl who had not lost her family after all. Then she made herself stand.

"I have to go back up," she told Rita. "She's still up there. She's still holding what's left. The city's coming through the night — Damián's got them, Renata's got the light, Teo's home — but *she's* still up there paying for all of it, and somebody has to be with her at the end." She looked at her grandmother, at the old sereno who had known the Sleeper as a laughing girl fifty years ago. "You told me the story, Abuela. Tell me the end of it. When she finally gets to sleep — when the last of it goes — what happens to the one who held it?"

Rita's old face was wet and unreadable. "In the story?" she said. "In the story, mija, nobody ever freed her. So nobody ever knew."

"Then I'll find out," Alba said, "and I'll come back and tell you, and you can tell the children." She kissed her grandmother, and her brother, and she went to the window, and she looked up the long hill at the Needle where the beacon had just gone warm and gold, and she closed her eyes, and she went down, and stepped sideways, one last time, into the drowsing city — to climb the tower and keep, at last, the promise her mother had run out of self to keep.

Behind her, for the first time in eleven years, her little brother lay down on his bed in the low gold light. And slept.

### Chapter Sixteen: The Round, and the Girl Who Walked It

The Duermevela that Alba stepped into now was not the one she had left.

She had gone down into a drowning city — dim, ringing, the dark coming on like snow, the wandering *velados* helpless and the nightmares blooming at every corner. She came up now into something she had no word for, because there had never been anything like it in eleven years to need a word. The drowsing streets were still dim, still soft-edged, still the city seen through lashes. But they were *warm* now. All along them, at every corner, in every window, glowed the low gold homing-lights Renata had lit through the whole grid — and by that light Alba could see the city coming through its night.

Everywhere, people were waking. Not fleeing — *waking,* the way you wake from a hard dream into a lit room and lie there a moment letting your heart slow and understanding, slowly, that you are safe. She passed a drowsing plaza where a hundred people who an hour ago had been strangers frozen in their separate terrors now stood together in the gold light, some weeping, some laughing at nothing, all of them pointing each other home. She passed the widow Paz — the real drifting piece of her, still in her housecoat, still with the little gold cross — and this time the widow was not drifting alone toward a door that would never open; a young man had stopped beside her, someone Alba didn't know, someone who had woken and turned back the way they all turned back tonight, and he was saying the words, *you're not lost, señora, look, there's a light, that's the way home,* and as Alba watched, the widow Paz turned her upturned face down from the dark at last, and found the light, and began, slowly, to come back. Alba stopped and pressed her hand to her mouth and wept for one second, no more, because she'd promised herself on the way in that if she ever found a way to bring them home she'd come back for every one — and here was the city doing it for her, for all of them, without being asked twice.

She passed a man teaching a child the words. She passed an old woman who had clearly just come through something terrible sitting on a step with her face in her hands and three younger people around her, not touching, just *there,* saying the thing, saying *you came back, you came back.* The nightmares were still there — Alba could see them, the great slow shapes, at the edges — but they were thinning. You could not keep a nightmare fed in a city that had decided, all at once, to look at its fears together in a warm room instead of alone in the dark. That was all a nightmare had ever needed to die: company, and a light on.

And through it all, everywhere, threading up from the streets and down from the rooftops and along the very light itself, ran the round — the voice, or now the ten thousand voices, calling the hours across the healing city.

She found Damián on a rooftop between the hill and the tower, and for a moment she just watched him, because she wanted to keep the picture. The loneliest boy in the world, standing over a city, cupping his hands to his mouth, calling the hour into the softening dark — and the city calling it back to him. He had never in his life belonged anywhere. He belonged here, tonight, utterly, the way a lighthouse belongs to a coast.

"You're supposed to be up the tower," he said, without turning, because he saw everything, always, and had seen her come. But his voice was full of something enormous and undone.

"I'm on my way up the tower." She came and stood beside him. Below them the healed city glowed. "I stopped to look. I wanted to see it once. In case." She didn't finish that. "You did this, Damián."

"I did the first street." His pale eyes were wet, and he was grinning like a fool, the exhausted radiant grin of someone who has finally, after seventeen years, been *useful* — has found the exact shape of the hole in the world that he and only he was built to fill. "*They* did the rest. Every single person I pulled out turned around and pulled out the next one. It went out from me like — like light, Alba. Like your grandmother's story, the ground going soft and restful under the girl's dreaming, except it was fear going soft under people just — *being there* for each other. I only had to show them once." His voice cracked. "Seventeen years I thought never sleeping made me broken. It made me the one who could walk into the drowning and not drown. You knew that. You looked at the worst thing about me and you called it a watch." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, roughly. "I don't know how to thank someone for that. I don't think there's a word."

"Then don't." Alba took his hand. Above them the Needle rose into the gold-shot dark, and its beacon — the last killing light, the one that had kept the Sleeper awake for eleven years — burned low and warm and steady now, Renata's doing, a lamp instead of a spear. "Walk your round. Call your hours. Get everyone through to morning." She squeezed his hand. "And when you call dawn, call it loud. Because I'll be at the top of that tower, and I want her to hear it. I want the last thing she hears before she finally gets to sleep to be a whole city telling each other *all is well.*"

Damián looked at her for a long moment. He had guessed, of course. He always guessed ahead of the words.

"When you free her," he said carefully, "when the last of it goes out of her and she finally sleeps — you don't know what it costs the one who's with her. Nobody's ever done it. Your grandmother said so. Your mother tried and it took her." His hand tightened to the point of pain. "I let you walk into the Duermevela because I could see the way and shout. I can't come up the tower with you, Alba. It's too far in — it's true sleep up there at the end, not the drowse, and true sleep is the one country I can never, ever enter. When you go into the deep part with her, I lose you. You'll be somewhere I have spent my whole life shut out of. And I won't be able to shout you home."

"I know," Alba said gently.

"So how do you come back?"

And Alba, who had been turning this over the whole climb, who had watched two million people tonight go into the dark and come back because someone had shown them the door and left a light on — Alba smiled, and it was her mother's stubborn smile, and she said:

"The same way everyone else did tonight. Somebody leaves a light on, and somebody calls the hour, and I follow it home." She let go of his hand, slow, finger by finger, the way you let go of something you fully intend to hold again. "So leave the light on, Damián. And when you call dawn — call it like you mean it. I'll be listening for you from the top of the world."

She stepped off the edge of the roof, and the Duermevela caught her — distances lie, in the drowse, and the distance from *here* to *with her at last* was, for the first time all night, not so very far — and she went up, up the warm gold spire, up the impossible stair, up toward the one chamber at the top of everything where a girl had been awake for a thousand years and was, at last, about to be allowed to close her eyes.

### Chapter Seventeen: The Sleeper's Dream

The chamber at the top of the Needle was quiet now.

The hundred lamps had gone gold. The great glass crown of the tower, that had blazed white over the city for eleven years, glowed low and warm, and in the middle of it, on the steel table, the girl who was also enormous lay with her frantic breathing finally slowing, her freed hands open at her sides, her eyes — for the first time in eleven years — still beneath their lids. Not asleep. Not yet. But no longer fighting. Waiting. The way you wait, at the very lip of sleep, in the duermevela, for permission to fall.

Alba climbed onto the table beside her, small against the vastness that was also just a seventeen-year-old girl, and took her hand, and the moment she did, she was somewhere else.

She was in the Sleeper's dream. A thousand years of it, all at once, the way dreams hold whole lives in a breath.

She saw the beginning — the girl from Rita's story, from the prologue every grandmother once told, the one who dreamed so deep her dreams went walking and made the ground restful. She had not been a goddess. That was the first lie the story told to make it bearable. She had been a girl, an ordinary frightened girl, who happened to be the best sleeper anyone had ever seen, in a hard age, in a hungry land — and when the travelers came and slept well on her dreaming and did not want to leave, they had not made a bargain with her. You did not bargain with a girl in those days. They had simply built on her, and around her, and finally over her, and told her it was an honor, and told her the city needed her, and told her — every generation, in every language the city ever spoke — that this was what she was *for.* That her rest belonged to everyone but herself. That a good girl carried what she was given and did not complain, and that what she had been given was everyone's dark.

A thousand years of it. Alba felt it all in the space of a held breath — the crushing, endless weight of every nightmare the city had ever refused to dream for itself, piled onto one back, dressed up as holiness so no one had to feel bad about the pile. The Sleeper had not been a wall the city built. She had been a girl the city *buried,* politely, over centuries, and called it a temple.

And Alba saw the small things too, which were somehow worse than the vast ones — because the vast weight of a thousand years was too big to feel, but the small things fit exactly inside her chest. She saw the girl learning, early, that if she woke and complained, the songs stopped and the keepers looked frightened, and so she learned not to complain, the way Alba had learned not to yawn in the street. She saw her being praised for her stillness, told she was good, told she was brave, told the city loved her — and understanding, slowly, across centuries, that the love was real and the cage was also real and that the city had found a way to make the two feel like the same thing, so that to be loved was to be kept and to be kept was to be loved, and there was no seam between them she could put her attention on and unmake. She saw a thousand years of a girl being thanked so beautifully for her suffering that she came to believe the suffering was the price of the thanks, and that if she ever put it down she would be putting down the only love she'd ever been given. That, Alba understood, was the real chain. Not the cistern. Not the lamps. The gratitude. They had bound her with gratitude, and it had held for a thousand years, because gratitude is the one chain a good heart will never try to break.

"They told me it was love," the Sleeper said. Her voice, here in the deep of her own dream, was just a girl's — tired past tired, but a girl's. "Every keeper who ever sat with me. Every sereno. They loved me, I think, in their way. They sang to me so kindly. And they never once, in a thousand years, asked if I wanted to put it down. Because if I put it down, they'd have to pick it up. And nobody, in a thousand years, wanted to dream their own bad dreams." She turned her head, and looked at Alba, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse. "Your mother came. Two years ago. She was going to climb up here and take it from me — carry it herself, so I could rest. She thought that was mercy. She thought one good woman could pick up what a whole city put down." A terrible tenderness. "It took her in a night. One good woman is not enough to carry what two million people won't. It was never a job that one person could do. That was always the lie. That was the lie that let everyone else off."

"I know," Alba whispered. "I'm not here to take your place."

The Sleeper went very still.

"Everyone who ever came was here to take my place," she said, "or to keep me in it. You're the first who came to do neither. So why *are* you here, sereno's child? What do you want from me, if not my post?"

"Nothing." Alba's eyes were streaming. "I don't want anything from you. I came because my mother ran out of self at the last step, and nobody should be alone when they finally get to sleep after a thousand years. I came to hold your hand." She gripped it tighter. "The city's got it now. All of it. Every person out there is dreaming their own bad dreams tonight, for the first time — theirs, just theirs, the right size, and they're getting through them, together, with a light on and each other's voices calling them home. They're picking it up. Two million small piles instead of one crushing one. You don't have to carry any of it anymore. Not one nightmare. Not one more night." She leaned close, the way she leaned close to Teo. "You can put it down. You can *sleep.* Real sleep. The deep clean dark you gave everyone else and never once got to have. I'll hold the door open. I'm the best sleeper in the whole city." A wet laugh. "I learned from the story about you."

For a long moment the Sleeper did not move. A thousand years is a long time to be told a thing is your purpose. It does not come off in an instant.

Then, slowly, the frantic under-thrum that had filled the chamber — the pressure Alba had felt as a pull from the streets far below, the thing that had said her name in the water — began to ease. The Sleeper was letting go. Alba could feel it, the great slow unclenching, a thousand years of held breath beginning, at last, to release. And she understood, in that moment, the true shape of the cost — the thing Damián had feared, the thing Rita couldn't name, the thing that had taken her mother.

The Sleeper had held the door between deep sleep and the waking world for a thousand years. She had been the wall, yes — but she had also been the *way.* The path down into true rest ran through her. And when she finally went through it herself, all the way down, into the deep clean dark she had earned — the door would close behind her. Someone standing at the threshold, holding it open, holding her hand, would either come back through it in the last instant before it shut, or would be on the wrong side when it did.

Her mother had held the door for the Sleeper and been on the wrong side when it — no. No. Her mother had never gotten this far; her mother had run out of self at the step below. Alba was further in than her mother had ever been. Alba was at the very threshold, holding the hand of a girl sinking gratefully into a thousand-years-owed sleep, and the door was beginning, slowly, to close.

"You should let go of my hand now, child," the Sleeper murmured, already half-gone, already sinking, her voice thick with the first true drowse she'd tasted in a thousand years, drunk on it, grateful past speech. "I'm going down. All the way down. You don't want to be holding on when I reach the bottom. The door shuts behind the last sleeper. It always has." A sigh like a whole city settling. "Thank you for the hand. Nobody ever... in a thousand years... nobody ever just held my hand and wanted nothing..."

The door was closing. The deep dark was rising. And Alba, on the threshold, in the last warm gold of the beacon, did not let go — not yet — because there was one thing left she had to do, one thing she had come two years and one impossible night to do, and she would not leave it undone even now, even here, even with the dark rising and the door swinging shut.

She had to let go of her mother.

### Chapter Eighteen: What Alba Gave

Her mother's dream was still there.

Alba had carried it for two years — the table, the blade, the mango, the beginning of a sentence — down into sleep three nights a week, the one door left to the one person she could not bear to lose. It was the whole reason she had never taken the light. It was the deepest, warmest, most well-worn place in the whole country of her sleep, and she knew, with the terrible clear knowing of the drowse, that if she wanted to come back through the closing door in time, she could not go alone and she could not go empty. She needed something to follow home. And she had spent tonight teaching a whole city the trick of it: you got through the dark by leaving a light on and calling the hour and following a voice home.

But she also knew, with the same clear knowing, the thing her mother's ghost had almost told her at the top of the tower. *You can't free her by taking her place.* You could not free the Sleeper — you could not free *anyone* — by refusing to let the dead be dead. She had been doing to her mother, in her own small way, exactly what the city had done to the Sleeper: keeping her, out of love, in a place she should have been allowed to leave. Two years of climbing down into a Sunday kitchen to hold a woman who was gone, because putting it down meant picking up the grief herself, awake, in the daylight, the right size, alone —

— no. Not alone. Never alone again. That was the whole thing she'd learned tonight. Not alone.

So Alba, on the threshold of the closing door, with a thousand-year sleeper sinking grateful into the dark beneath her hand, did the hardest thing she had ever done, which was not to save anyone. It was to let someone go.

She called up her mother's dream — the kitchen, the Sunday light, the blade going *tock tock tock,* her mother looking up, starting to say something — and this time she let it finish. She let the sentence complete. *Don't come after me,* her mother had said at the top of the tower, the true end of the two-year sentence. *And don't stay down here with me either,* Alba finished it for her, in her own voice now, because that was the rest of it, the part her mother had been too kind to say. *Live up there. In the light. Awake. With the ones who are still warm. Come visit me the ordinary way — in memory, in the daylight, over a piece of fruit — not by climbing down into the dark to hold a door open on a room I've already left.*

"Adiós, Mamá," Alba said, in the warm gold light, at the top of the world. "I kept you as long as I could. I have to put you down now. I have to go be awake." She kissed the memory of her mother's cheek, the scarred thumb, the Sunday light. "*Te quiero. Y me voy a casa.*" I love you. And I'm going home.

She let the dream go. She felt it lift off her like a weight she'd carried so long she'd stopped feeling it as weight and only felt it now, unbearably, as it left — and in its place, in the deepest warmest room of her sleep, there was a lamp, low and gold, left on. A light to follow home. She had traded her mother's dream for a way back. It was the worst trade of her life and it was the right one, and both of those would be true forever.

She knew, even as she made it, that she would never dream that particular dream again. That was the cost, the real one, the one no one could see from the outside and no one would ever be able to thank her for. For two years the mango kitchen had been hers to visit — imperfect, fragmentary, always leaving, but *hers,* a door she could open three nights a week. She was closing it now, herself, with her own hand, because a door you keep open to the dead is a door that keeps you from the living, and she had a brother who slept in the waking world now and a boy waiting for the dawn and a whole life up in the light that she had spent two years too grief-drunk to properly walk into. You did not get to keep everything. That was the last thing the tower taught her. You put down what you were carrying so that your hands were free to hold what was still warm, and it broke your heart, and you did it anyway, and that — not the never-sleeping, not the nightmares, not the machine — that was simply what it cost to grow up in a city that had forgotten how to grieve.

And now she could hear it — faint, far below, threading up the warm gold spire through the deepening dark, a voice. His voice. Calling the hour, calling it *loud,* calling it like he meant it, the way she'd asked, so that a thousand-year sleeper and a girl on a threshold could both hear a whole city telling itself the truth at last:

"*¡Las seis de la mañana!*" Six in the morning. Damián's voice, cracking with exhaustion and joy, and behind it two million voices, the round gone round the whole city and come back changed. "*¡Y todo, todo está bien! ¡Ya viene el alba! ¡Vuelvan a casa, todos! ¡Ya viene el alba!*" And all, all is well. The dawn is coming. Everyone, come home. The dawn is coming.

The door was almost shut. The Sleeper was gone, all the way down, into the deep clean dark she had earned across a thousand years, and — Alba felt it, and wept with relief — she was *sleeping,* truly sleeping, at last, at peace, released, the whole crushing weight put down and carried off in two million willing hands. Free. The girl the city buried was, at the very last, simply asleep, like anyone, like everyone, safe.

And Alba let go of her hand.

She let go, in the last instant, and turned toward the light she'd left burning and the voice calling the hour, and she followed them the way she'd taught a whole city to follow them tonight — not fleeing the dark but coming up through it, toward the warm gold lamp and the boy calling *ya viene el alba* — come home, come home — and the door of the deep swung shut behind her, soft as a mother closing a child's bedroom door, and Alba surfaced —

— gasping, alive, on the steel table at the top of the Needle, in the ordinary waking world, in the low gold light of a beacon that no longer needed to burn. Beside her the great glass crown was empty. The Sleeper was gone — not dead, Alba knew, with the bone-deep certainty of one dreamer for another, but *gone home,* dreaming her own dreams for the first time in a thousand years, wherever the deep clean dark takes those it finally sets free.

And through the glass, over the whole hushed city of Vísperas, the sky in the east was doing something it had not properly done in eleven years.

It was going dark. And then — beginning, at the edge — to go light again. The right way. In order. Night, and then dawn.

The city was asleep. And the sun was coming up on it.

### Chapter Nineteen: Dawn

They found each other on the rooftop, of course. Where else.

Alba came down from the Needle in the gray-gold hush of the first true dawn Vísperas had seen since before her brother was born, and the city she came down into was *silent* — not the ringing silence of the Duermevela, but the real, soft, ordinary silence of a place where people are sleeping. The hum was gone. She had never once in her life heard the city without the hum, the great low electric note of the light that never went off, and its absence was so total it was almost a sound of its own. All along the hill, in every window, the lamps had gone gentle and low, and behind them, Alba knew, people were lying down. The dominoes had stopped on the corners. The three-shift schools had gone dark. The buses stood still. For the first time in eleven years, Vísperas had nothing more urgent to do than sleep, and it was doing it, block by block, hill by hill, two million people lying down in the dawn and closing their eyes and falling, at last, clean into the dark and back out the other side.

Damián was on the rooftop, their rooftop, and when he saw her come up over the parapet he made a sound she felt in her own chest, and then his arms were around her and hers around him, and they held on, wide awake, in the growing light, and neither of them said anything for a long time because there was nothing that needed saying that the holding on didn't say better.

"You came home," he said finally, into her hair.

"You left the light on," she said. "You called the hour. I followed you home. Just like you said." She pulled back to look at him, at the pale eyes that had seen everything, always, all his life. "Damián. She's free. She's sleeping. A thousand years and she finally — " Her voice went. She tried again. "The city's got it now. All of it. Their own dreams, the right size, together. She doesn't have to carry it anymore. Nobody has to carry all of it anymore. It's just — everybody carrying a little. Which is how it should have been the whole time."

"I know." He was crying too, unashamed, the exhausted radiant tears of the longest night in the history of the world. "I felt it change. Around three, maybe four — the round stopped being something I was doing and started being something the city was doing to itself. I just kept calling the hours after that because —" he laughed, wet — "because I liked it. Seventeen years I hated being awake. Tonight I got to be the one person awake for the exact right reason, in the exact right place, for one whole night, and it was the best night of my life, and I never want to do it again, and I'd do it every night forever if you asked me to."

Alba looked at him. And she remembered, then, the thing she'd half-decided at the top of the tower and hadn't let herself finish deciding until now, with the dawn coming up and the whole city going down into its first sleep.

"You've never slept," she said slowly. "Your whole life. Seventeen years. Because as long as she was awake up there, kept awake, the wall down, the whole city couldn't — but especially you. You were born the morning she was taken. You're the most awake person who ever lived because you were the closest to her waking." She took his face in her hands. "But she's asleep now, Damián. The wall's healed. Everyone in the city is sleeping right now, this minute, for the first time in eleven years." Her thumb moved over his cheekbone, under the sleepless eyes. "Which means, for the first time in seventeen years — so can you."

Damián went absolutely still.

"I don't know how," he whispered. It was the most frightened she had ever heard him, this boy who walked a city of nightmares without flinching. "Alba. I've never — I don't know how. Nobody ever taught me. I don't know how to —"

"I know how," Alba said gently. "It's the one thing I've always known how to do. And I've never once gotten to do it for someone who actually wanted it more than anything in the world." She drew him down, there on the rooftop, in the first dawn, the sky going pink and gold and *ordinary* over the sleeping city. "Lie down. Put your head here. Close your eyes — I know, I know, you never close your eyes, close them anyway, just this once, I've got you. There's a light on. I'm right here. I'm not going anywhere. I'll leave the light on and I'll call the hour and I'll be right here the whole time, and if it's dark, it's only dark, and you can always come back, and I will always be here when you do." Her voice was breaking and she kept going, the sereno's words, the only words that had ever worked. "You spent one whole night making sure two million people weren't alone in the dark. Let me make sure of it for you. Just this once. Just tonight. *Duérmete, mi amor.* Sleep. I've got the watch."

And Damián — the boy born awake, the last sereno, the loneliest boy in the world who had spent seventeen years shut out of the one country everyone else got to visit — laid his head in the lap of the girl who could still dream, and closed his eyes, and after a moment, a long trembling moment, while she held him and the dawn came up and the whole city slept around them —

— for the first time in his entire life, Damián slept.

Alba kept the watch over him as the sun rose. She did not sleep herself; someone had to keep the light on, someone had to be awake to call the hour, and she found she didn't mind, she found there was no place in the whole waking world she would rather be than exactly here, awake, at dawn, watching over the sleep of someone she loved. That was what a sereno was, in the end. Not one girl buried under everyone's dark. Just someone who stays awake, a little while, so someone else can rest. Anyone could do it. Everyone, taking turns, could do it forever.

Below them, Vísperas slept.

Above them, the sun came up.

And the beacon at the top of the Needle, the light that had never once gone off in eleven years, flickered gently in the strengthening day, and — no longer needed, no longer a spear, no longer a jailer — quietly, for the first time since a girl was carried up into it and a boy was born beneath it, went out.

## Epilogue: La Ciudad Que Duerme

It is three years later, and I am keeping the watch, and I have time now to finish the story I began.

I told you at the start that the last good night's sleep in Vísperas was eleven years ago, and that a child was born the next dawn who never once closed his eyes, and that I married him. All of that is true. Damián sleeps now — he has slept every night for three years, and he still, every single time, gets the same astonished look on his face just before he goes under, as if he cannot quite believe the country is real and they are going to let him in again. I never get tired of watching it. It is my favorite thing in the waking world. He told me once, half-asleep, that in his dreams he sometimes walks a city his mother described to him in the dark when he was small, and that it is beautiful, and that he is never lonely in it — and I did not tell him that I think it is the Duermevela, gentled now, healed, given back to him as the country it should always have been. Some doors you do not need to explain to the person walking through them. You just leave the light on, and let them go. I leave the light on, and I call the hour, soft, just for him, and he closes his eyes, and he goes home, and I keep the watch until he comes back. Some nights I sleep too, curled against him, and dream my own ordinary dreams, the right size, mine. But someone should always keep a watch. I learned that from a girl who kept one for a thousand years, and I have tried to learn the better lesson too, which is that no one should ever have to keep it alone, or for longer than a night, or without being thanked.

Here is what became of everyone, since you have come this far and it would be unkind not to tell you.

Teo is fourteen now, and impossible, and alive. He sleeps like a boy who is making up for eleven lost years, which is to say he cannot be got out of bed before noon, which is to say he is a completely ordinary teenager, which is to say he is a miracle so total that Rita and I sometimes just stand in his doorway and look at him breathing. He does not remember the fog. He remembers the mango, though. He asked me to make it, the hedgehog, the Sunday after the longest night, and I did, and I cried the whole time, and it was the first time I had touched a mango in two years, and it was the first time in two years I thought of my mother and did not have to climb down into the dark to find her — she was just there, in the kitchen, in the blade going *tock tock tock,* in the daylight, the right size, where the ones we lose are supposed to live. I put her down, up at the top of the tower, and it turned out that putting her down was the only way I ever got to properly carry her.

Rita tells the story now. The real one — not the one grandmothers used to tell to frighten children into their beds, but the true one, about a girl who was buried politely for a thousand years and called a temple, and the city that finally learned to dream its own bad dreams so she could rest. She tells it in the plazas, on the corners where the old men used to play dominoes at three in the morning with their glassy sleepless eyes, and the children listen, sleepy, blinking, *sleepy* — do you understand what a wonder that is, a sleepy child in Vísperas — and then they are carried home to bed. She says she is training the next serenos. There are hundreds of us now. It is not a bloodline and it was never a bloodline; that was another lie the old story told, that only certain families could keep the watch. Anyone can keep the watch. You only have to be willing to stay awake a while so someone else can rest, and to trust that someone will do the same for you. The whole city takes turns now. We call it *la ronda* — the round. Everyone walks it, a little. No one walks it alone.

Renata Vela is not in prison, which some people think she should be, and I understand them, and I disagree. She dismantled the Needle's light array with her own hands — would not let anyone else do it — and turned Vela from a company that sold the light that kept you awake into something stranger and smaller and better: a company that makes lamps, ordinary lamps, low and gold, the kind you leave on for a child so the dark has no door in it. She funds Rita's serenos. She comes to the storytellings and sits at the back and does not speak. Once, after, she found me and said, "I keep thinking I should feel forgiven, and I don't, and I've decided that's correct — that the grief is the point, that I'm supposed to carry it awake now, the right size, forever, like everyone else." Then she said, "Marisol would have liked your husband. She was afraid of the dark too." And she went home, to grieve, awake, with a lamp on. Come through it. On the far side. It is the bravest thing I know, and she does it every day, and no prison could ask more of a person than that.

And the Sleeper?

I do not know, and I have made my peace with not knowing, and I think that is right too. She is not under the city anymore, and she is not at the top of the tower, and she is not anyone's temple or anyone's engine or anyone's job. Sometimes, on the deep nights, when I let myself go all the way under, I think I feel her — very far off, very faint, patient the way stone is patient, but *easy* now, unclenched, dreaming her own dreams at last, wherever the deep clean dark takes the ones it finally sets free. I like to think she sleeps well. I like to think that after a thousand years of dreaming everyone else's nightmares, she gets, now, only the good ones. A kitchen. A Sunday. A blade going *tock tock tock.* Someone looking up, and finishing the sentence.

They used to say a city that never sleeps is a city that never has to stop, never has to grieve, never has to lie down in the dark and trust the morning to come. They sold us that. We believed it for eleven years, and it nearly killed us, all of us, one sleepless dawn at a time.

But a city that never sleeps is only a city that has forgotten it is allowed to rest.

We remember now.

It is dawn again over Vísperas — the real kind, the ordinary kind, the kind that comes after a night — and the lamps are going gentle and gold all down the hill, and the whole city is asleep, and my husband is dreaming beside me with that astonished half-smile, and somewhere a grandmother is carrying a sleepy child to bed, and somewhere a woman is grieving with a lamp on, and somewhere, very far down, a girl who slept for a thousand years is finally, finally resting.

And I am awake, and I am keeping the watch, and all is well.

*Ya viene el alba.*

The dawn is coming.

It always does, now.
