# La Casa Que Respira
### *The House That Breathes*

**A Novel**
*by Esperanza Vidal Roca*

---

> *"The dead do not leave. They simply learn to be quiet."*
> — Abuela Consuelo, on the night she stopped speaking entirely

---

## Part One: La Llegada (The Arrival)

### Chapter One

The house had been waiting for Marisol Reyes-Ortega for thirty-one years — since before she was born, since before her mother had fled it with nothing but a cardboard suitcase and a rosary she'd later lose in a Bogotá bus station. It waited the way the Andes wait: without urgency, without doubt, in the absolute certainty that all things return to altitude eventually.

She arrived in October, which in the valley of Huaracaña means the rains have not yet decided whether to be gentle. The taxi driver from the pueblo refused to take her past the iron gate. He did not explain why. He crossed himself twice, accepted her crumpled bills, and reversed back down the red dirt road so quickly that the rear tires spat mud across the hem of her white linen skirt — the skirt she had chosen, absurdly, because her therapist in Medellín had once told her that wearing white was an act of intention.

She stood at the gate with her suitcase and looked up at the house.

It looked back.

That was the only way she would ever be able to describe it afterward — that the house *looked*. The windows were wrong for it, arranged too symmetrically, four up and four down, like eyes that had learned to blink in pairs. The bougainvillea had eaten most of the lower facade, and through the gaps in its purple riot she could see the original paint: a yellow so pale it was almost white, almost bone.

Her great-aunt Dolores had died inside three weeks ago. No one had found the body for eleven days.

According to the notary in Bogotá, Marisol had inherited everything.

She pushed open the gate. It did not creak. It opened as smoothly as breath.

---

### Chapter Two

The notary had sent photographs, but photographs had manners. They cropped out the smell: woodsmoke and something older beneath it, something that reminded Marisol of the biology lab in her university, of the particular sweetness that precedes rot. The photographs had also failed to capture the *sound* of the house — a low, periodic settling that she heard on her first night, lying in the room that had been her mother's as a girl, a sound not quite like pipes and not quite like breath but located, unmistakably, in the walls.

She told herself: old houses speak. Architecture has grammar. This is masonry cooling in night air.

She told herself this until almost three in the morning.

On the dresser across the room, her great-aunt Dolores's things remained exactly as they had been. A hairbrush with silver bristles. A glass of water, evaporated now to a white mineral ring. Three photographs in matching oval frames: one of the Virgin of Chiquinquirá, one of a man Marisol didn't recognize, and one — the one that made her sit up in bed when the moonlight finally reached it — of a woman who wore Marisol's own face but twenty years older, standing in front of a house that was not this house, in a landscape she had never visited, in a dress the color of the rains.

She turned on the light.

The third photograph was of Dolores herself. Of course it was. A trick of the dark, the moonlight, the altitude.

She did not sleep again before morning.

---

### Chapter Three

The pueblo of Huaracaña had 340 people and one of everything: one church, one pharmacy, one telephone, one school, one man who repaired small engines and resented it. It had, according to the woman who ran the market stall adjacent to the pharmacy, three reputations: for its aguardiente, for its orchids, and for the Reyes house on the hill.

"Your family's house," the woman said. Her name was Petra and she had the look of someone who had decided years ago to simply say everything plainly. "People here call it *la casa que respira*."

"The house that breathes," Marisol translated, needlessly.

"Your great-aunt Dolores lived there alone for forty years after your grandmother left." Petra weighed out Marisol's rice without looking at the scale. "She said the house kept her company."

"And the people in the pueblo? What did they say?"

Petra sealed the rice in a paper bag and held it out. Her expression was the expression of a woman who had given this answer before and expected to give it again.

"We said: *yes, but what kind of company?*"

## Part Two: Las Raíces (The Roots)

### Chapter Four

In 1962, Marisol's grandmother — Abuela Consuelo, the woman she had known only as a very old silence in a rocking chair — had been the youngest daughter of Don Abelardo Reyes, who had built the house in 1931 with money that no one in Huaracaña could account for, given that Don Abelardo had arrived from somewhere unspecified with no discernible trade and an accent that locals described, variously, as coastal, interior, foreign, and *wrong*.

Don Abelardo had brought with him two things besides the money: a wife named Perpetua who would not leave the property once it was completed, and a religion that was not quite Catholicism. The priest from the pueblo had visited three times. After the third visit he told his congregation only that the family on the hill had "their own arrangements with God," and he did not elaborate, and he did not go back.

Marisol found all of this in the house's archive: a cedar chest beneath the staircase, packed with letters, ledgers, a land deed, and a journal that was not quite a journal — more a record of agreements, written in a hand that grew increasingly ornate as the pages progressed, as though the writer were slowly becoming someone more formal than they had started out.

The last entry, dated June 1961, read:

*The house is satisfied. The house is always satisfied when we remember what we owe it. Consuelo does not understand yet. She will.*

Consuelo had fled in November 1961. She never came back. She never spoke of the house. She raised Marisol's mother in Medellín, in a second-floor apartment, and she requested in her will to be buried in the city, not in Huaracaña's churchyard.

Marisol sat with the journal on the floor of the archive room for a long time.

Above her, from somewhere deep in the house, came the sound she had heard her first night — that low, satisfied settling.

She thought: *the house is breathing*.

She thought: *it has been breathing for ninety years*.

---

### Chapter Five

Father Miguel from the Huaracaña church was younger than she expected and uncomfortable in the way of someone who had received a better education than his post required. He came when she asked, which surprised her, and he walked through the front gate without hesitating, which surprised her more.

"You're not afraid of it," she said.

"I'm a guest," he said. "The house hasn't decided about me yet."

She stopped walking. "What does that mean?"

He looked at the facade with the expression of a man choosing words carefully. "This valley has a long history of what the Church calls *animism* and what the people here call *being polite to things that are older than you*." He paused. "The Reyes family made a specific kind of arrangement when this house was built. I've read the diocesan records. The priest who came here in the early years — he wasn't disturbed by what he found, exactly. He was disturbed by how *content* everything seemed."

"Content."

"The house. The land. The family." He finally looked at her. "Things that want something are usually angry about it. Things that have what they want are very, very calm."

That night, cooking alone in the kitchen, Marisol cut her palm on a tin lid and bled into the sink. She ran the water and watched the blood dilute and swirl and disappear down the drain.

The house made its sound.

It was quieter than usual. Almost gentle.

Almost, she thought, like satisfaction.

## Part Three: La Sangre (The Blood)

### Chapter Six

She began to dream in the house's grammar.

Not nightmares — she had expected nightmares. Instead she dreamed in the language of inventory: room by room, object by object, the house showed her where things were. The broken tile behind the wardrobe in the south bedroom. The water stain beneath the kitchen flagstone where something had leaked in 1978 and never been properly repaired. The cedar chest's false bottom, which she had not found.

She found the false bottom on a Thursday morning, cold and clear, with the orchids in the garden opening in the early light. Inside it: a clay figure, female, no taller than her thumb, worn smooth in the way of very old things that have been handled by many hands. And beneath the figure, a single folded paper.

The paper read, in her great-aunt Dolores's careful script:

*For the one who comes after. You are not inheriting a house. You are inheriting a relationship. The house will show you what it needs. The house has always been reasonable. Your grandmother was afraid of reasonable things. You do not have to be. — D.R., September 2024*

Marisol sat on the floor of the archive and held the clay figure and felt, spreading through her chest like the particular warmth of aguardiente, something she could only describe as recognition.

The house breathed.

She breathed with it.

---

### Chapter Seven

She called her mother from the one telephone that still worked in Huaracaña — her cell signal reached the pueblo but not the hill, which she had decided was not an accident.

"You need to leave that house," her mother said. She said it the way she had always said the things she was most afraid of: quickly, flatly, without punctuation, as if speed might prevent the words from meaning too much.

"Mamá. Tell me what Abuela Consuelo told you."

A long silence. In it, Marisol heard all of her mother's forty years of careful distance from this valley, this altitude, this particular red dirt.

"She said the house chose people," her mother finally said. "She said it chose her and she refused it. She said refusing it was the hardest thing she ever did and that she was never entirely sure she had succeeded."

"What does it mean, to be chosen?"

Another silence. Longer.

"She said the house wanted someone to stay. Someone to keep the agreement. Someone to be — she used a word, *guardiana*. Keeper. She said the land had a hunger and the house was its mouth and the family was its teeth and if you fed it willingly it would never need to take."

"And if you didn't feed it willingly?"

Her mother's voice, when it came, was very small and very old.

"She said: look at how Dolores died. Eleven days before anyone found her. But she died peaceful, Marisol. She died in her own bed with her hands folded and a look on her face that the funeral home director described to the notary as — " her mother stopped.

"Tell me."

"*Satisfied*," her mother said. "He said she looked satisfied."

## Part Four: La Guardiana (The Keeper)

### Chapter Eight

Marisol planted the clay figure in the garden, at the base of the oldest bougainvillea, where the roots were thick as her wrist and the earth was soft and perpetually damp. She did it at dawn, in her white skirt, which was no longer an act of intention so much as a habit she had not gotten around to abandoning.

She had made her decision somewhere between her mother's phone call and the third morning she had woken to find the orchids had arranged themselves — not moved, she was careful about the word *moved* — arranged themselves in a pattern outside her window that was not the pattern they had been in the night before.

She was not afraid.

This was the thing she kept returning to: the fear she had expected had never arrived. In its place was something she had no good word for in Spanish or English, something that lived in the body below language — a sense of fit, of correspondence, of a lock and its key finally occupying the same room.

She thought of her great-aunt Dolores, alone here for forty years, and did not pity her.

She thought of her grandmother Consuelo, in her Medellín apartment, sleeping on a second floor, never in a house, never on ground — and understood, for the first time, not why she had run, but what she had spent forty years running *from*, and why she had never been able to stop.

The house breathed.

Marisol pressed her hands into the red dirt and breathed with it.

---

### Chapter Nine: *Epilogue — One Year Later*

Petra from the market stall comes to the house on Tuesdays now, with vegetables and news from the pueblo. She never comes inside. She leaves things at the gate and Marisol comes down to collect them, and they talk for a few minutes about ordinary things — the aguardiente festival, the school's leaking roof, the particular behavior of the cloud cover on the eastern ridge.

Petra has never asked why Marisol stayed.

She has, once, near Christmas, said: *the house seems quieter this year*. And then, after a pause: *content*.

Marisol smiled and said yes, she thought it was settling in.

Petra nodded as though this was the most natural thing in the world, which, in Huaracaña, it was.

Father Miguel visits once a month. They drink coffee on the front steps, which he considers close enough to inside to count, and they discuss the diocesan records, and sometimes the nature of agreements, and once — only once — the question of what it means to be chosen by a thing that is older and more patient than fear.

"Do you think it's evil?" he asked, that one time, looking up at the windows.

Marisol considered the question with the care it deserved.

"I think it's hungry," she said. "I think it was always hungry. I think the difference between a haunting and a home is whether anyone stays to feed it."

She looked up at the windows.

The windows looked back.

---

*Above the valley of Huaracaña, in the dry weeks before the rains, the orchids bloom in colors that have no reliable names. The house on the hill is yellow, almost white, almost bone. Its bougainvillea is very old and very purple and in the evenings, when the light comes low off the western Andes, the house seems to move — seems to expand and contract with something slow and deep and rhythmic.*

*The people of the pueblo say: the new Reyes woman is strange, but polite.*

*They say: she tends the garden.*

*They say: the house, this year, seems happy.*

*They say nothing else. They have learned, over ninety years, the etiquette of not asking too many questions of things that have what they want.*

*La Casa Que Respira* — an original novel in the tradition of Latin American Gothic fiction, drawing on themes of generational inheritance, indigenous animism, and the horror that lives not in malevolence but in appetite.
