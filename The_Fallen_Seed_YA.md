# The Fallen Seed

## Part One: The Prophecy

### Chapter One: The Bridge

The temple didn't look like a temple anymore. It looked like a bank.

Selene had lived inside it for two years, and she still couldn't get used to that. Once, a very long time ago, this had been a place with columns and firelight and people who came barefoot up marble steps to ask the gods what to do with their lives. Now the marble had been sanded flat and reclad in glass, and the columns held up a lobby with leather chairs nobody sat in, and the eternal flame — the one that was supposed to have burned since before the city had a name — had been rewired into a flickering electric thing behind frosted glass, just bright enough that tourists who wandered in by accident would take a photo and feel like they'd seen something old.

None of them ever noticed the door behind the flame. The one that didn't lead to an office. The one that no elevator went to.

Selene noticed it every single day, because on the days the god called for her, she was the one who had to walk through it.

Tonight was one of those days.

"You don't have to do this," said Marcus, hovering at her shoulder the way he always did — half a step behind, hands twisting, a grown man frightened of a seventeen-year-old girl because of what sometimes spoke through her. He was the temple steward. His whole job was to keep her safe and fed and available, and he did it the way you'd care for an expensive machine you didn't understand and were terrified of breaking. "Jupiter's office can wait until morning. I'll tell them you were unwell."

"Jupiter's office hasn't waited for anything in three thousand years," Selene said. She was already pulling off her shoes. The god-voice didn't come to the well-dressed; that was the first rule the last Oracle had taught her, before the role wore the woman down to nothing and passed, like a fever, into Selene. "I don't think it's going to start with me."

The stone floor was cold under her bare feet. It was always cold. She'd stopped expecting it to be anything else.

"Selene." Marcus's voice dropped. "You're only seventeen."

"So was the last one when she started." Selene didn't look at him, because if she looked at him she might lose her nerve, and she couldn't afford to lose her nerve, not with the god already pressing at the edges of her mind like a storm circling a coastline, waiting for permission to come ashore. "That's the whole point of us, isn't it? They pick us young because the young ones bend instead of break. For a while."

She stepped through the door before he could answer.

The chamber on the other side was smaller than anyone expected. People imagined a throne room when they heard the word *oracle* — gold, incense, a choir of voices you couldn't see. It was nothing like that. It was a bare stone room the size of a closet, with a basin of black water sunk into the floor and the smell of copper and old rain hanging in the air. The only light came from the water itself, a faint restless shimmer, as if something at the bottom of the basin was very slowly turning over in its sleep.

Selene knelt at the edge the way she'd been taught. Not in prayer — the Oracle didn't pray. Prayer was for people who wanted the gods to listen. The Oracle was the thing the gods spoke *through*, whether she liked what came out or not.

She took a breath. She held it. She put her hands into the water.

The visions never came gently. That was the second rule, and the one nobody could teach you; you just had to survive learning it. They came like a door kicked open in a house you'd thought was empty. Tonight was worse than usual. Tonight she felt two presences at once, and they did not belong to each other.

The first was Jupiter — she knew him by now, the way you know a particular kind of weather, that pressure behind the eyes, that sense of a very large and very old attention turning its full weight onto you, curious and cold and used to being obeyed. His attention lived at the top of the world. It came *down* onto her.

The second presence came *up*.

It was fainter. Older. Patient in a way Jupiter's court never was, the way tree roots are patient, the way stone is patient. It rose through the black water from somewhere so far below the city that Selene's mind flinched from the distance of it, and it carried words she did not want to hear.

*The son has risen.*

The words came up through the water like they'd been sitting at the bottom for years, waiting for someone to finally look down and find them.

*Fed on the dark fruit. Marked by the realm his father lost. He will do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it.*

Selene ripped her hands out of the basin.

Water ran down her wrists and off her fingertips, and it clung to her skin longer than water should, as if it didn't want to let go, as if it wanted to keep touching her. Her heart was slamming. Behind her she heard Marcus's sharp intake of breath, and she knew that her face had already told him everything — that this was not a routine message about crop prices or a rival family or which foundation Jupiter should bless with his favor this quarter. This was the other kind. The kind that got people killed for the crime of having heard it.

"Selene." Marcus's voice had gone thin as paper. "What did the god say?"

And here was the thing she would think about for a long time afterward, in the middle of the night, when she couldn't sleep: for half a second — half a heartbeat — she almost lied.

She didn't know why. She had never met the son the god was talking about. She didn't know his name, his face, whether he was cruel or kind, whether he was a danger or a boy who happened to be standing in the path of a very old fear. She knew nothing about him at all. And still, some animal part of her, some part below thought, wanted to close her mouth around the prophecy and swallow it and protect a stranger she would never meet.

She didn't. She was seventeen and she had followed the rules her whole life, because following the rules was the only thing that had ever kept her safe, and old habits held.

"Get me Hercules," she said instead. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Jupiter needs to hear this. And he needs to hear it from someone he'll believe."

Marcus fled to make the call.

Selene stayed kneeling by the black water for a long moment, alone, her wrists still wet, and she looked down at her own reflection in the shimmering dark. A thin girl with tired eyes and hair coming loose from its braid. Seventeen years old. The fourth Oracle in twelve years, because the god burned through them young.

*The son has risen,* the water whispered again, quieter now, almost gentle, almost like a warning meant only for her.

"I heard you the first time," Selene told it.

She did not yet know that she had already begun to dream of him.

Three floors up and half a city away, in an office with a view he never bothered to look at, Hercules was cleaning a blade he didn't need.

It was a habit older than most countries. The weapons had never been the part that mattered — his real weapons weren't the kind you could sharpen by hand — but there was something steadying in the ritual of it, the cloth and the oil and the slow careful motion, and steadying was a thing Hercules found himself needing more often lately, though he'd have died before admitting it to anyone in the tower.

He had been an agent of the throne for longer than he could easily count. Long enough that the counting itself had stopped meaning much. He remembered — dimly, the way you remember a dream — being a very young half-god with a very human, very frightened mother, and he remembered being told that if he served the throne well enough, faithfully enough, *long* enough, the line between demigod and god might one day blur. Might, eventually, disappear. That he might be allowed, at the end of it all, to climb his own pyre and burn away the mortal half and rise up a true god, and take a seat among them instead of standing behind their chairs.

*Eventually.* That was the word Jupiter always used. Hercules had been living inside *eventually* for three and a half thousand years.

His phone buzzed against the desk. A single line, routed through channels that didn't officially exist, from a number that officially wasn't there:

*The Oracle has a name for you.*

Hercules set the blade down.

He looked, for a moment, at the ceiling of his office — at the smooth pale surface of it — as if Jupiter's penthouse, nine floors above, might somehow be visible through all that steel and stone if he only wanted to see it badly enough.

"A name," he said, to no one. "Finally."

He'd been waiting a long time for a name. A name meant a hunt, and a hunt meant service, and service meant one more brick laid on the road to the pyre. He didn't let himself feel the thing that might have been eagerness. He'd learned not to feel that. Eagerness, in a servant of the throne, was just disappointment that hadn't arrived yet.

He picked the blade back up and finished cleaning it, because the ritual wasn't done and he liked to finish things.

He did not know, could not have known, that the name belonged to a face he would, before the end of all this, have every reason in the world to spare and no permission at all to.

He did not know that the same would soon be true of the Oracle.

### Chapter Two: The Floor That Wasn't There

Kael found out his father was alive in the same week he found out his father owned the building he'd been mopping the lobby of for two years.

He was eighteen, which the paperwork said made him an adult, though nobody had ever treated him like one and he'd never much felt like one either. He had a night job cleaning a glass tower downtown, the kind of job that paid in cash and asked no questions, which suited him because he had no answers. No diploma. No parents anyone could name. A first name that fit him and a last name that had changed three times on three different sets of forms because no one, including Kael, had ever been entirely sure what it was supposed to be.

The tower was one of those buildings that seemed taller at night than in the daytime. Forty floors of dark, empty offices stacked over a lobby of black marble, and Kael had come to know that lobby better than he knew his own face. He knew where the marble was veined like frozen lightning and where it was smooth as still water. He knew which of the recessed lights flickered, and which had burned out, and which the building would never bother to replace because no one important came through at three in the morning except the boy with the mop.

And he knew about the crack.

It ran across the floor near the elevators — a hairline seam in the marble, thin as a strand of hair, that no polish and no filler and no amount of scrubbing had ever been able to close. Kael had reported it once, in his first month, and the building manager had looked at him like he'd reported the weather. *It's an old floor. Old floors crack. Mop around it.* So he mopped around it. But he had never quite been able to shake the feeling — a feeling he'd never told a living soul, because it sounded insane — that the crack didn't stop at the bottom of the floor. That it kept going. That it went down, and down, past the foundations and the pipes and the bedrock, into somewhere the building had no idea it was built on top of.

He was on his knees over that crack, wringing his mop into the bucket, when the marble began to bleed cold.

Not water. Not damp. *Cold* — an arriving cold, specific and directional, the way the temperature of a whole room changes when someone opens a door two rooms away that you didn't know was there. Kael went still. He put his palm flat against the marble and felt it come up through the crack: a draft, faint but real, rising out of a seam that had no business having a draft at all, carrying a smell he had no word for and recognized immediately, all the way down in his bones. Old rain. Turned earth. And underneath those, faintly, something sweet — sweet the way fruit is sweet right at the edge of going bad, right before you understand what you're actually smelling.

"You polish his floors," said a voice behind him, "and he never even told you your own name."

Kael didn't jump.

That was the strangest part, and he'd think about it a lot, later. His body had spent eighteen years learning to flinch — flinch at raised voices, flinch at the sound of the front door, flinch at a hand moving too fast in the corner of his eye — and now, when a stranger spoke out of nowhere in an empty lobby at three in the morning, his body simply declined. It didn't flinch. It turned, slow and calm, as if it had been expecting this.

An old man stood in the middle of the lobby, where thirty seconds ago there had been no one at all.

He was dressed like a lawyer from a decade you couldn't quite place — a good dark coat gone soft and shiny at the elbows, a collar from another era. And his eyes were wrong. Not the wrong color. The wrong *nothing*. Where a person's eyes should catch the light and hand a little of it back, his caught nothing, held nothing, gave nothing — like looking down a well at noon and finding no bottom to throw the sun back up at you.

"My name," Kael said carefully, "is Kael."

"Your name is Kael," the old man agreed, with a small nod, as if Kael had passed a test. "Kael, son of the man they told you was dead. My name is Verus, and I keep the doors for your father, and he would like — at long last, after a very long wait, and your father knows more about waiting than anything else alive — to finally meet you."

"My father's dead."

"Your father is *exiled*," Verus said, gently, almost kindly, "which the living so often mistake for the same thing." He gestured at the crack in the floor. "But you've always known there was a door here. Haven't you. You've felt it under your hand for two years. You just never had the key."

Kael's mouth had gone dry.

Because it was true. It was exactly, precisely true — he *had* felt it, every night, the sense of a door under the floor — and he had never told anyone, not the manager, not his mother before she died, not the priest who'd stood over her grave and told him she was in a better place. He had told no living soul. And here was a stranger with no bottom to his eyes, saying it back to him like a fact everyone knew.

"You knew my mother," Kael said. It wasn't a question, quite.

Something crossed the old man's face — old, and sad, and quickly gone. "I knew *of* her. Your father loved her more than the living will ever believe the dead can love. He let her take you and hide you and raise you as a mortal, and he let you think he was dead, because the alternative was letting his enemy find you. He gave you up to keep you breathing. That is the whole story of your childhood, Kael, and I'm sorry no one was allowed to tell it to you until now."

The crack in the floor was widening as he spoke.

Not violently. Patiently. The way a wound opens when you finally stop pretending it's closed. Cold poured up out of it, and that sweet turned-earth smell rolled thicker, and far down — impossibly far, farther than the building, farther than the ground — Kael could see a light that wasn't light. A dim red glow, like the color you see behind your own closed eyelids when you tip your face up to the sun.

He should have run. Even then, some part of him knew he should run.

Instead he thought of his mother at the end, in the narrow hospital bed, her hand thin as paper in his. *Your father isn't who you think*, she'd said, and then the medicine had pulled her under before she could finish, and Kael had spent two years assuming she'd meant something small and human and disappointing. A liar. A coward. A man who'd walked out. Something ordinary.

He stood up. His knees popped. The cold rose to meet him like it had been waiting his entire life.

"What does he want," Kael said. "My father. What does he actually want from me."

"One meal," said Verus. "That's all he's asked me to offer you. One meal at his table. If you don't like what you find down there, I will walk you back up these stairs myself, and you can polish his floors until the sea comes and takes the whole city, and no one will ever bother you again." The old man's not-eyes held him. "But I don't think you'll want to go back up. I think you have been hungry your entire life, Kael, and never once been told the name of the thing you were hungry for. Come and eat. And find out."

Kael looked down at the crack, at the widening dark, at the red not-light far below.

He thought: *there was never going to be a night I walked back up these stairs and clocked out and went home to an empty apartment and did this again tomorrow. There was never going to be that night. There hasn't been that night in two years. I just didn't have the door.*

"One meal," he said.

And he stepped down into the cold.

### Chapter Three: One Meal

The first lie Kael had to unlearn was that the underworld was fire.

It wasn't. It wasn't fire, and it wasn't screaming, and it wasn't the crude butcher's-shop horror the living painted on their walls to frighten each other into behaving. It was a country. A vast, quiet, grey country, and the terrible secret about it — the one nobody ever warned him about — was that it was *beautiful*.

There was a river in it the color of poured pewter, wide and slow and utterly still, so still it looked less like water than like a mirror someone had laid flat across the floor of the world. There were fields of pale flowers that whispered when the wind moved through them, a soft papery sound like a crowd of people all deciding, at once, not to speak. There was a great grey hall built into the root of a mountain, and the mountain had no top, because it went up and up into the underside of the living world and simply kept going.

And it was beautiful the way the hour before dawn is beautiful — when the whole world is holding its breath and hasn't yet decided whether to let it go. Kael walked through it beside Verus and felt something in his chest that he had no name for, something that was almost peace and almost grief, and he understood that he was going to have to be very careful here, because a place this beautiful could make you forget to be afraid.

His father was waiting in the hall.

Kael had built a hundred versions of this man in his head over eighteen years — hero, villain, coward, ghost — and none of them had prepared him for the truth, which was that his father looked *like him*. Tall. Ageless in the way old mountains are ageless, worn smooth rather than worn down. He had Kael's jaw and Kael's hands and Kael's way of holding his shoulders, and his eyes were the deep bruised red of a pomegranate seed held up to the light.

"You have your mother's mouth," the god said. His voice was very quiet, and the whole hall seemed to lean in to catch it. "I'm glad. I wouldn't have wanted you to have only me."

"You let me think you were dead," Kael said. It came out harder than he meant it to. He'd promised himself in the descent that he'd be calm, and he wasn't calm.

"I let you think you were safe." His father didn't flinch from it. "There is a difference, and it cost me more than you will ever forgive me for, and I would do it again tomorrow. Sit, Kael. My name is Pluto, though I have a hundred others, because your species was too afraid to say the true one out loud. There is exactly one thing on this table worth your time. After that, you may decide everything else for yourself. I won't stop you. I've done enough deciding for you already, and none of it while you could see me do it."

There was a long grey table, and it was bare except for a single object set in front of the empty chair.

Kael sat, because his legs had stopped taking suggestions from him, and he looked at the thing on the table and understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that his whole life had been a hallway with this table at the end of it.

It was a pomegranate.

Split along one seam — split, he realized, the exact way the marble in the lobby had split. And inside, the seeds weren't red. They were darker than red, garnet going to black, wet and gleaming with a faint light of their own, and the smell coming off them was the same one he'd first breathed up through the crack in the floor: old rain, turned earth, and that sweetness underneath, pouring off the seeds now like heat off summer stone.

His mouth watered. He hated that it watered.

"What is it," he said.

"It is the oldest coronation in the world," Pluto said. "Older than crowns. Older than the idea of kingship. When Persephone ate of this realm — you may have heard that story; the living tell it wrong — it did not trap her here. That's a tale they tell to feel sorry for a queen who chose. It *marked* her. It said to the dark: *this one is ours now, whatever else she is also.* That is all this fruit does, Kael. It marks. It tells the deep places that you belong to them." He folded his ancient hands on the grey table. "Eat, and you are my son in more than blood — you are heir to everything my brother Jupiter took from me when he threw me down here three thousand years ago for backing a throne that lost. Don't eat, and you are a mortal boy who once had a strange dinner, and Verus walks you home, and by the next new moon you will have forgotten the taste of this and gone back to your life. I will not lie to you about the choice. I have lied to you about everything else, your whole life, to keep you alive. I will not lie to you about this one."

"Then don't," Kael said. "Tell me the cost. There's a cost. There's always a cost. Even I know that much, and I've never had anything worth a cost before."

Something moved across the god's face — pride, maybe, or grief, or both at once.

"It changes the eyes first," Pluto said quietly. "Then the appetite. Then the loyalty. It will give you a hunger, Kael, and the hunger will feel exactly like justice, and it will be the hardest thing you ever do — the hardest thing — to tell the difference between the two before it's too late to matter. Some who eat it never learn the difference at all. They spend the rest of their long lives certain that every hungry thing they do is right." He met his son's eyes. "That is the cost. Everything worth having down here has one. That's the only honest thing about the dead: we never pretend a thing is free."

Kael looked at the fruit.

He thought about the lobby floor, the crack he could never close, the two years of feeling a door under his hand. He thought about his mother's unfinished sentence. He thought about eighteen years of being a boy nobody claimed, doing a job nobody watched, in a building — he understood now — that his own father owned, owned without ever once coming down the stairs to say his name.

He thought: *there was never going to be a version of tonight where I didn't eat it. I just wanted someone to tell me the truth first. And he did. He's the first one who ever did.*

"Okay," Kael said.

And he picked up the fruit, and he ate.

The seeds were sweeter than anything living had ever tasted, and colder, and under the sweetness was iron — the specific mineral tang of blood — and it wasn't until the third seed that Kael understood he was crying. He didn't know if it was because the taste was that good or because he could feel something happening, some quiet, permanent door swinging shut somewhere deep inside him, a door his mother had died trying to hold closed, closing now forever, gently, without a sound.

When he looked up, his father was watching him. And Kael watched the god's face change — watched something move through it that he could only much later find the words for: recognition, and reunion, and a terrible aching tenderness, the look of a man seeing his own reflection across three thousand years of exile and finding it, at last, looking back.

And from the way his father looked at him, Kael knew — before he ever saw a mirror, before he ever caught his own face in the still pewter river — exactly what color his eyes had just become.

### Chapter Four: The Storm and the Machine

Selene dreamed of him before anyone told her his name.

It started the night after the prophecy, and it did not stop. She'd close her eyes in the narrow cell they gave the Oracle — a beautiful little prison, silk sheets, a lock that only worked from the outside — and she'd find herself standing on a grey shore beside a river the color of poured metal, and there would be a boy there with his back to her, throwing flat stones out across the water. The water never rippled. He kept throwing anyway.

She never saw his face. She saw his shoulders, and the tired set of them, tired in the exact place she carried her own tiredness. She saw his hands. And once — only once — she heard him speak, low and rough, throwing a question up at the grey underside of the underworld's sky:

*Is there anyone down here who ever got to choose?*

Selene woke every time with her chest aching and her face wet, and she told no one. Not Marcus. Not the temple. Not, God, *not* Jupiter's office. Because she understood — with the specific, cold dread of a girl who had spent two years being a door other people walked through — that the dreams were not a message from the god. They didn't have that feeling, that kicked-open-door violence. They were soft. They were *hers*. And a door was not supposed to want anything. A door that started wanting things was a door about to be replaced.

---

There was a girl in the temple called Iris, and Selene loved her, and that was the most dangerous thing about her.

Iris was eleven. She had come to the temple the way they all came — chosen, not asked, delivered by a family who had been made to understand that a great honor was being done to them and that the honor was not the kind you could refuse. She had a gap in her front teeth and a way of asking questions that no one ever answered, and Marcus was already, quietly, beginning to call her *the next one*, which meant the temple had looked at Selene and started doing the arithmetic. Four Oracles in twelve years. The god burned through them young. Someone was always being kept in reserve, fed and taught and readied, so that when the current door wore through, there would be another one standing by, already shaped to the frame.

Selene had worked out what Iris was for in the girl's second week, and had been trying to protect her from the knowledge ever since, which was impossible, because you cannot protect a person from a thing by refusing to name it. You can only leave them to find it out alone, in the dark, the way Selene had.

"Does it hurt," Iris asked her that night. She'd padded into Selene's cell after the rite, in her nightgown, the way she did when the temple was too quiet to sleep in. "The god-voice. When it comes. Does it hurt."

Selene considered lying. She was getting a great deal of practice, lately, at considering lying.

"Yes," she said instead. "It hurts. Not like a cut. Like — you know when you've been carrying something heavy, and you finally put it down, and your arms shake? It's the shaking. Something moves through you that's too big for you, and after, you shake, and you're tired in a place sleep doesn't reach." She pulled the girl up onto the narrow bed beside her. "But you have years yet before you have to know that firsthand. Years and years. Don't let Marcus tell you otherwise."

"He says I'm gifted."

"He says that to everyone he's frightened of losing money on," Selene said, and Iris laughed, the gap-toothed laugh, and Selene held onto the sound of it the way she'd later learn Kael held onto the memory of his mother's — as a thing that could be spent, and lost, and never quite bought back.

Because here was the truth Selene could not say to an eleven-year-old with a gap in her teeth: the temple did not love them. The temple *used* them, gently, the way you use a good tool, and when the tool wore out the temple grieved for exactly as long as it took to bring the next one in from reserve. There had been an Oracle before the one who'd taught Selene, and one before her, and one before her, and Selene did not know a single thing about any of them — not their names, not their faces, not where they were buried. They had been doors. Doors don't get remembered. Doors get replaced, and the house goes on exactly as before, and no one stands at the new door and thinks about the old one.

*I have to find him,* Selene thought, holding the sleeping girl, staring at the ceiling of her beautiful little prison. *I have to find the boy the god showed me, and point Hercules at him, or Jupiter will pull the levers he has on me — and the lever he has on me is asleep on my arm right now with a gap in her teeth. He doesn't even know it yet. But he'll find it. He finds everyone's.*

It was the first time she understood, all the way down, that she was not the only one the throne could reach through. That there was no version of loyalty that kept everyone she loved safe. That the price of being a good door was that everyone who leaned on you became a place the world could push.

She did not sleep. In the morning she braided Iris's hair and sent her to lessons and told no one anything, and she carried the new knowledge around inside her all day like a stone she'd swallowed, and it was still there, cold and heavy, when the summons came from the tower.

Jupiter's penthouse took up the top nine floors of the tallest tower in the city, and it didn't so much have windows as it simply *was* windows — walls of glass on every side, so that the city spread out below like a circuit board of light, small and orderly and answerable, laid at the feet of the man who had ruled it for three thousand years.

He did not look like a tyrant. That was the thing Selene could never make peace with, in all the times she was brought before him. He looked like the best version of everyone's favorite uncle: silver at the temples, warm-eyed, with a laugh that made you desperate to have been the one who earned it. When they brought her in that night, he crossed the whole vast room to take her cold hands in his warm ones, as if she were a beloved niece home from a long trip and not a seventeen-year-old asset flown in to help him kill someone.

"Selene." Her name in his mouth was a gift he was choosing to give her. "You look exhausted. They work you far too hard down in that temple. I'll speak to Marcus."

"I'm all right, my lord."

"You're *not*," Jupiter corrected, gently, steering her toward a chair with a warm hand at the small of her back, "you're worn to the bone, and you're brave, and you've brought me the worst news I've had in a hundred years, and you brought it to me anyway, because you're loyal. I don't forget loyalty, Selene. I'm the oldest thing in this world that still remembers when loyalty was *invented*." He smiled. "Now. Tell me the god's exact words. Not your summary of them. His words. Every one."

And behind him, at a long glass table, Juno watched.

Selene had met the Queen of Heaven exactly twice before, and both times she'd come away with the same impression: that Jupiter ruled the sky, and Juno ruled Jupiter, and only one of the two had ever noticed. She was a handsome, still woman with a tablet in front of her and a pen she never used, and she was, Selene had slowly worked out, the one who actually read the reports. Jupiter *performed* the throne. Juno *ran* it. And she was watching Selene now with an attention so total and so patient that Selene had to look away from her just to keep her own voice steady.

"'The son has risen,'" Selene recited. "'Fed on the dark fruit. Marked by the realm his father lost. He will do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it.'"

The room did the thing rooms do around gods when the mask slips.

The light dimmed. The glass fogged with a sudden inside-out cold. And far out over the sleeping city — miles off, above a clear and cloudless sky — a single spear of lightning stood straight up from the ground and did not fall, held there, quivering, like a held breath, like a fist that hadn't decided yet whether to open.

*The one before it,* Selene thought, watching the storm move behind Jupiter's warm eyes. *The son does to the throne what the throne did to the one before it. And he knows exactly which one before it. He's been afraid of that one sentence for three thousand years.*

"Hades," Jupiter said. He set down the crystal glass he'd been holding, very carefully, as if it might otherwise shatter in his grip. "My brother. The Rich One. He's raised himself a son at last. Marked him. Put him on the board." He turned to look out over his city. "Do you know why I put my brother in the dark, Selene? The real reason. Not the version the temple sells the tourists."

"No, my lord."

"Because he *waited*." Jupiter's reflection in the black glass was smiling, and there was nothing warm in it at all. "Everyone else who ever came for my throne came *shouting*. My own father came shouting — I could see him coming from a mile off. But my brother just sat. And waited. And was patient. And one day I understood the only real truth about power that there is: a patient enemy is the only kind that ever actually wins. The loud ones tire themselves out. The patient one is still there when you're tired. So I put him where patience couldn't reach me — down in the dark, to rule a kingdom of ghosts, and told him to be grateful I hadn't done worse." He turned back, and his warm face had gone strange. "And now, three thousand years later, he has reached up through the floor of my own city and put a knife in it. And he's given the knife my own family's face."

"My lord—"

"Find me the boy." Jupiter crossed the room again, and took her cold hands again, and this time there was frost forming on his — a cold that burned where it touched. "You are the door the god speaks through. So you will kneel at your black water, Selene, and you will *find* my nephew, and you will tell Hercules where he stands, and Hercules will end this before it can begin, and you will have saved the whole world from a war it doesn't even know is coming. That is what an Oracle is, isn't it? A finger that points. So point. For me. There's a good girl."

Behind him, Juno finally set down her pen.

"Husband," she said. It was the first word she'd spoken all night. Her voice was low and dry and carried the whole weight of six thousand years of cleaning up after him. "The girl is not a hound. Don't speak to her as if she were one. She'll do it better if she believes it was her own idea."

Jupiter laughed, delighted, kissed Selene's frozen knuckles, and swept off toward his private rooms like a man who had solved a difficult problem simply by handing it to someone else. The frost melted off Selene's hands slowly, painfully, like feeling coming back into something that had been asleep.

Juno rose. She crossed the enormous room and stood in front of Selene and looked at her with those still, reading eyes, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped so low it was almost gentle, which frightened Selene far more than any of Jupiter's frost.

"You dreamed of him," Juno said. "Didn't you. Before you knew the name. Before tonight."

Selene's blood turned to water. "I don't—"

"I was an oracle's granddaughter, child, before I was ever anybody's queen. I know the look of a girl the god is showing something to that isn't meant for the throne to see. It's all over your face." Juno's expression didn't change. "So I'll give you the only piece of advice anyone in this building will ever give you for free. You are standing directly between two brothers who have hated each other since before your species learned to bury its dead. And whichever one of them wins — and one of them always wins — an Oracle who *chose a side* is an Oracle remembered forever as a traitor. So point where you're told to point." She straightened, smoothing her dress. "And whatever it is you're dreaming — stop."

She walked away.

Selene sat alone in the cold, enormous room, looking out through the glass at the city that didn't know it was owned, and she understood two things with total, sickening clarity.

The first was that she was going to have to find him. There was no way out of that. The god had shown him to her, and Jupiter had commanded it, and there was nowhere on the board for an Oracle who refused.

The second was that she was already, God help her, afraid of the moment she would.

### Chapter Five: Everything Down Here That Ever Got to Choose

For a while — and this shamed him later, but it was true — Kael was almost happy in his father's country.

He'd never been given anything in his life, and the underworld gave with both hands. Verus taught him the grey roads, how they moved, how to walk them without getting lost in the folds of the dark. The shades — the quiet dead, drifting through the fields of pale flowers with their old faces and their softened, faraway griefs — did not fear him. They *bowed*. They called him *young lord*, and something in Kael that had been starving for eighteen years ate that up and immediately, shamefully, wanted more, and he hated how much he wanted it, and he wanted it anyway.

There was one shade, in that first happy while, that Kael thought about long after.

She was an old woman — or she had died old; the dead wore the age they'd carried at the end — and she was lost. Verus explained it to him as they walked: some of the dead came down clear-eyed and settled easily into the grey fields, but some arrived tangled, still gripping the last hard thing that had happened to them, unable to put it down and rest. This one had been walking the same short stretch of the fifth road for longer than Verus could say, back and forth, back and forth, looking for a door in a wall that had no door.

"What's she looking for," Kael asked.

"Her son," said Verus. "She thinks he's just ahead. He is not just ahead. He is eighty years old and still living, and she died before she could tell him a thing she'd meant to tell him, and now she cannot rest until she does, and she never will, because he is not here, and the telling is not a thing the dead are permitted." The old doorkeeper said it without cruelty, the way he said everything — as simple fact, the honest currency of the dead. "She'll walk that road until the road itself wears out. There's nothing to be done. You learn, down here, to walk past."

But Kael did not walk past.

He didn't decide to stop, exactly. He just found himself kneeling in front of the old woman, on the grey road, the way he'd one day kneel in front of far worse and far older things, and he said, "Tell me. Whatever it was. Tell me, and I'll carry it up, and I'll find him, and I'll say it to him. I go up top most nights. I can find one old man." He didn't know if it was true. He wanted it to be true. "Tell me what you needed him to know."

The shade stopped walking.

She looked at him — really looked, the first thing in this grey country besides Selene that had ever looked at Kael like he was a person and not a young lord to bow to — and her old face crumpled, and she told him. It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing. It was not a secret or a fortune or a last confession. It was only that she was proud of him, her son, that she'd always been proud of him and had been too proud herself, too stiff, too much her own hard mother's daughter, to ever once say the word out loud while she was alive to say it. *Tell him I was proud. Tell him I'm sorry I made him wait until I was dead to hear it.*

"I'll tell him," Kael said. "I promise."

And the shade put down the thing she'd been carrying — he saw it happen, saw the weight go off her the way weight would one day go off a god — and she smiled, and she stepped off the road she'd worn into the ground, and she walked out into the pale fields and lay down among the flowers and was, at last, at rest.

Kael stood up.

And felt something leave him.

He didn't know what it was, that first time. He only knew the shape of the hole: a warmth, gone cool; a memory that had been there a moment ago and now was a room he couldn't find the door to. Something to do with cold water. Something to do with a hot day. He reached for it and it wasn't there, and Verus was watching him with those bottomless eyes, and Kael understood, all at once, standing on the grey road where an old woman had finally put her burden down, exactly what his father had meant.

*It changes the eyes first. Then the appetite. Then the loyalty. And it does it through kindness. Every good thing you do down here costs you a piece of the boy who wanted to do it.*

"You didn't have to do that," Verus said. Not unkindly. "You spent something you can't get back, on a stranger, for nothing anyone will ever thank you for."

"I know," said Kael. He looked out at the fields where the old woman rested. "I'd do it again."

"I know you would," said Verus, and something almost like grief crossed the old doorkeeper's face, there and gone. "That's the thing your father is most afraid of, and most proud of, and can't say either one out loud. Come. Walk on. And, Kael—" he paused "—you never did find her son. You couldn't have. The dead can't send messages up; you knew that when you promised. But you gave her the door anyway. You let her put it down. Sometimes the mercy isn't keeping the promise. Sometimes the mercy is being the person who was willing to make it." He walked on into the grey. "Remember that. You'll need it, at the bottom of things."

And the fruit was working in him. Exactly as his father had promised.

His eyes had gone first. He'd seen them in the still pewter river the second morning — garnet-dark now, his father's eyes set in his mother's face, a color no living boy had ever had. Then the appetite: he found he no longer needed to eat the food of the living, and worse, that he no longer *wanted* to. The memory of bread, of coffee, of his mother's terrible burnt eggs on a Sunday morning — these had begun to feel like a language he used to be fluent in and was slowly, word by word, forgetting.

And beneath those two changes, the third one was moving. The loyalty. The hunger that felt like justice.

Because Kael had asked, and Verus had told him. The whole of it. What Jupiter had done to Saturn — the grandfather Kael would never meet, cast down into a dark below even this dark, so deep that not even the god of the dead could reach him, chained there for three thousand years for the crime of being a king who wouldn't step aside for his son. What Jupiter had done to Pluto — his own brother, exiled to a kingdom of ghosts, told to be grateful. And what Jupiter still did, every single day, up in his glass tower: ruling mortals like a herd of animals, answering their prayers only when it flattered him, letting a hundred small cruelties fall on a hundred small people because it cost him nothing and reminded everyone, always, who they should be afraid of.

And the hunger in Kael looked at all of that and said, in a voice that sounded exactly like his own, perfectly reasonable: *Someone should stop him. You could be the one who stops him. Wouldn't that be justice? Wouldn't that be right?*

It was so hard to tell the difference. His father had warned him and it was still so hard. Because it *was* right, wasn't it? Jupiter *was* cruel. Saturn *was* wronged. Someone *should*—

He was on the grey shore, the night the two halves of this story first touched, throwing flat stones out across the pewter river just to watch them refuse to ripple, because it was the only thing down here that still behaved like the living world had behaved, and he needed, badly, to touch something that did.

"Is there anyone down here," he asked the vast grey sky, not expecting an answer, because there never was one, "who ever got to choose?"

"I don't know," said a girl's voice, behind him. "I was hoping you could tell me. I've been trying to work that out my whole life."

Kael turned around.

She was standing on the shore where no living thing was supposed to be able to stand — and he knew, instantly, that she was living, knew it the way he now knew cold and roads and the names of the dead. Her heart was beating. Her breath made the only fog on that whole grey shore. She was warm, and she was *here*, which was impossible, and she was looking at him with an expression he had absolutely no defense against.

Recognition.

"You," she said. Her voice shook, then steadied, the way a hand steadies when it makes up its mind. "I've been dreaming about the back of your head for a week. I thought I was making you up." A small, wrecked laugh escaped her. "You're supposed to be a monster. They sent me down here to find a monster."

"I ate a fruit that's turning me into one," Kael said, because he was too tired to lie and because something about her face made lying feel obscene. "So you might have the right man after all. Who sent you?"

"The throne."

"Then you should run."

"I know." She didn't move. "That's the strange part. I've done exactly what the throne told me to do for two years. I'm the Oracle. I'm the door the gods talk through. I found you so I could point Jupiter's hunter at you." Her voice cracked on the next part, and Kael watched her let it — watched her *choose* to be seen cracking, instead of performing that she was fine — and something in his chest that the fruit hadn't reached yet turned over and looked at her. "But I've been standing here for a full minute now, waiting to feel like I found a monster. And instead I feel like I finally caught up to the person from my dream who was always standing exactly where I was too tired to stand."

They looked at each other across three feet of grey stone and three thousand years of a war neither of them had started.

"What's your name," Kael asked.

"Selene."

"Ask me the question you actually came down here to ask, Selene," he said. "Not the throne's question. Yours. Your own. I'll tell you the truth — I've got nothing left worth spending a lie on."

And Selene — who had been chosen and never asked, who had spent two years relaying a god's will in a voice that wasn't her own, who had never once in her whole life had anyone stand in front of her and *wait* for a real answer — felt something break open in her chest that had nothing to do with any god at all.

"Do you want his throne," she asked, "or do you just want to stop being afraid of the man who sits on it?"

Kael went very still.

Because it was the exact right question. The one his father hadn't asked. The one Verus never would. The one the hunger inside him had been carefully, gently steering him away from every single day since he'd eaten the fruit. *Do you want the crown, or do you just want to stop being hunted?* Those were not the same thing. The fruit wanted him to believe they were the same thing. The fruit had a very good, very reasonable argument ready for why they were the same thing.

"No one," Kael said slowly, "has ever asked me what I want. In eighteen years. Not once. You're the first."

"That makes two of us," Selene whispered.

And far, far above them — three thousand miles up, through the whole thickness of the world, in a tower of cold glass — a hunter of the throne received a set of coordinates that the Oracle did not remember giving. Pulled from her sleeping mind by a queen who read every report and trusted no one's choices but her own.

But down on the grey shore, neither of them knew that yet. Down on the grey shore there was only a living girl and a boy dying slowly into something else, and a river that refused to move, and the first honest question either of them had been asked in years, hanging in the cold air between them like a held breath that neither one wanted to be the one to let go.

### Chapter Six: The Hound

Hercules found the crack in the lobby floor at three in the morning, and he stood over it for a long time before he went down.

He wasn't afraid. He had not been afraid in so long that he'd genuinely forgotten the shape of the feeling. But he was — and this was new, this was a splinter he kept catching on — he was *reluctant*, and reluctance was a thing he had no place to put. His whole self was built around a single load-bearing certainty: that the throne was right. That service was the road. That if he did the terrible, necessary things faithfully enough, for long enough, the door that had stayed shut to him for three and a half thousand years would finally open, and the pyre would be lit, and he would rise.

The coordinates in his phone glowed. *The fifth river. The grey shore.*

He went down through the widening seam, and the cold took him, and he walked out into the grey country with his real weapons coiled and waiting under his skin, and he found the boy on the shore of the pewter river, exactly where the coordinates said he'd be.

The boy was not alone.

The Oracle was with him.

Hercules stopped at the edge of the whispering flowers and watched the two of them for a moment before either noticed him, and what he saw undid something in him that he would spend the rest of this story trying, and failing, to tie back up.

They weren't touching. They were sitting a careful arm's length apart on the grey stone, and the boy — the marked one, the prophesied son, garnet-eyed and half-turned already into something that wasn't mortal — was *listening* to her. Head bent. Absolutely still. Listening the way Hercules had spent three and a half thousand years secretly, silently, wishing that Jupiter would once — just *once* — listen to him. And the Oracle was talking with her hands, and then she laughed at something, a real laugh, tired and surprised, the laugh of a person who hadn't expected to laugh again. And the monster Hercules had been sent to put down was watching her laugh like she was the first warm thing he'd touched since he died into whatever he was becoming.

*This is going to be a problem,* Hercules thought.

Then Kael's head came up.

He'd caught the wrongness in the air — the cold that wasn't the underworld's cold, the smell of old leather and something colder underneath it — and he was on his feet and standing between Selene and the dark before Hercules had taken a second step. Fast. God-fast. The fruit had given him that already.

"Oracle," Hercules said, stepping out of the flowers. "The throne would like its finger back. You've done your pointing. Go home."

Selene's face, over Kael's shoulder, went white — and then, to Hercules's genuine surprise, went *hard*. "I didn't tell them where he was."

"No," Hercules agreed. "You didn't. The Queen took it out of your sleep. You should learn that about the people you serve, girl — they don't wait for you to choose. They reach in and help themselves to whatever you've got." He drew one of the coiled weapons up out of his own forearm, and it came into his hand as a length of something that was neither metal nor fire, something that drank the dim light out of the air around it. "Son of Hades. My name is Hercules. And I'm sorry — I truly am; you seem far less of a monster than I was promised, and that always makes this part worse — but there is a prophecy that says you'll do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it. And I've been sent to make certain that particular wheel never gets to turn."

"Then we want the same thing," Kael said.

Hercules paused. "...What?"

"I don't want the throne." Kael's voice was steady, and behind him Selene made a small sound, because she knew what those words cost — knew that he'd only found the answer to them an hour ago, on this shore, when she asked. "I ate the fruit that's supposed to make me want it. I can feel it in me right now, telling me to take it. And I'm telling you — and I'm telling her, and I'm telling myself out loud so I have to actually hear it — I don't want to be the next Jupiter. I want to stop being hunted by this one. Those aren't the same thing. Everyone in this whole story wants me to believe they're the same thing." He didn't raise a weapon. He raised his empty hands, palms out. "You want the wheel to stop turning. So do I. So put that away, and let's be the two people in three thousand years who actually *meant* that."

And Hercules — the believer, the hunter, the man whose entire self was built on the certainty that the throne was right and the work was holy — felt the splinter go all the way in, down to the bone.

Because he had just heard the marked one, the prophesied threat, offer to stop the wheel — in the very same breath that Hercules understood, all at once and far too late, that *he was the wheel*. He was Jupiter's arm. Every son who had ever risen, every rival, every patient enemy — Hercules was the thing the throne sent to keep the wheel from turning, and it had never, in three and a half thousand years, occurred to him that keeping the wheel from turning and keeping *Jupiter* on top of it were the exact same act. That he wasn't protecting the world. He was protecting one man. One man who had promised him a fire he was beginning, in the pit of his stomach, to suspect would never, ever be lit.

"Get out of my head," Hercules said, and it came out ragged.

"I'm not in your head." Kael's garnet eyes were steady on him. "You already knew. You've known for three and a half thousand years. I can just smell it on you now — that's another thing the fruit does; I can smell everything. You reek of a promise nobody's ever going to keep."

The weapon in Hercules's hand flickered.

For one long moment on the grey shore, the entire future of the throne balanced on the reluctance of one very tired demigod. Selene held her breath. Kael held his empty hands out. And the river, behind them, did not ripple.

Then Hercules let the weapon sink back down into his arm.

"I'm not going to let you walk up to that throne," he said. "Understand me. If you *go* for it — if you reach for that crown — I put you in the ground, prophecy or no prophecy, feelings or no feelings. I've killed better men than you for less, and I'll do it clean, and I won't lose sleep, because I don't sleep." He looked, for a moment, terribly old — older than the city, older than the war. "But. I'm going to go back up there tonight, and I'm going to tell the throne I couldn't find you. And I have never lied to Jupiter in three and a half thousand years." He turned to go, and stopped, and did not look back. "So you should both understand exactly what it means that I'm about to start. Oracle. The Queen reads everything. Whatever you think you're going to do with what you feel for the boy — she already suspects it. Be faster than she is, or be gone."

He walked back into the whispering flowers and was gone.

Selene let out the breath she'd been holding. Beside her, Kael's empty hands had started to shake, now that the moment was past — the fruit-hunger, denied its throne, turning over restless and hungry in his chest.

"He's going to lie to a god," Selene said. "For us. Why?"

"Because we're the first people who ever offered him a way to stop being what he is." Kael turned to look at her, and for a moment the garnet eyes were almost gentle. "Same reason you didn't run when you found me. Same reason I didn't take the crown when you asked me the question." He swallowed. "It's a very small club, Selene — the people in this story who ever got to choose anything. I think it just went up to three members."

## Part Two: The Fruit

### Chapter Seven: The Cost of Every Kindness

The trouble with refusing a hunger, Kael learned, is that it doesn't go away. It just gets clever.

The first time Selene came down on purpose — not pulled through a dream, but choosing it, awake, with her own two feet — she nearly didn't make it, and the nearly taught them both something.

The crack in the lobby floor was not a door for the living. That was the whole trouble. Kael could open it from below, could fold the grey roads up to meet the marble, but a living body did not belong on those roads, and the underworld knew it, and pushed back. Selene stepped down into the widening seam that first night and felt the cold close over her like water over someone who had never learned to swim — felt her own heartbeat, the one warm mortal drum of it, become suddenly enormous and wrong and unwelcome, a sound the whole grey country turned to stare at. Her lungs forgot the trick of breathing. The dark leaned in to drink the heat off her. She was, she understood with a calm that was itself a symptom, beginning to die, gently, the way a candle dies when you close it in a jar.

And then Kael's hand closed around hers.

"Breathe out," he said. His voice was very steady, and very close, though she couldn't see him. "All the way. Empty. Good. Now — don't breathe in the dark. Breathe *me*. I'm cold, I'm the underworld's, the country won't fight what comes off me. Breathe what I breathe out and it'll let you stay. I've got you. I won't let go. Breathe."

She breathed him. Cold, and iron, and pomegranate. And the enormous wrongness of her own living heartbeat quieted, folded itself down small, became something the grey country was willing to tolerate as long as she was tethered to a thing that belonged there. As long as his hand was around hers.

That was the mechanic, and neither of them missed what it meant. She could only be in his country if she was holding onto him. The moment he let go, she'd start to die again. It should have frightened her. It did frighten her. But it also did something else, something she turned over for a long time afterward: it made the choosing physical. Every night she came down, she was choosing, with her actual body, to put her survival in the hand of the boy she'd been sent to destroy. There was no way to pretend it was duty. There was no way to pretend it was accidental. You do not accidentally breathe someone else's breath to keep your heart from stopping. You decide to. Over and over. On purpose.

"You could have just let me find that out the hard way," she told him, when they were sitting safe on the shore and her heartbeat had settled. "You could have let me nearly die and then rescued me. Big dramatic moment. Very heroic."

"I don't want to be the thing that rescues you," Kael said. "I've spent my whole life being nobody's rescue and nobody's anything. If we're going to do this — whatever this is — I want you to always know exactly what it costs and exactly what you're choosing. No surprises. No moment where you look back and realize you were tricked into needing me. I'd rather tell you the price up front and let you decide, every single time, than have you one day."

"That's the second time you've told me a price up front," Selene said slowly. "The fruit told you the price up front too. Your father. He said it was the one honest thing about the dead — they never pretend a thing is free."

"Yeah." Kael looked out at the still water. "I think it's the only thing I actually got from him worth keeping. That, and the eyes." He almost smiled. "Everybody up top spent my whole life pretending things were free. The job was free — turned out it cost me my whole self, four years, no future. Being nobody's kid was free — turned out it cost me a father. Down here at least the bill comes with the meal." He turned to her. "So here's your bill, Selene, every night, in writing: you can only be here if you hold on to me, and the day you stop wanting to hold on, you should stop coming, because I will never once make it hard for you to leave. That's the deal. That's the whole deal. Still want to come down tomorrow?"

"Yes," Selene said, without hesitating, and watched something in his garnet eyes ease that she hadn't known was clenched.

He learned this over the strange suspended weeks that followed — the season when the throne believed him lost, and Hercules's lie held, and Selene came down the crack in the lobby floor two and three nights a week, telling Marcus she was walking to clear her head, telling the temple she was resting, telling herself a story about duty that got a little thinner every single time she descended.

They talked, on the shore, about small things — which was itself a kind of miracle, because neither of them had ever had anyone to spend small things on.

Kael learned that Selene had been eight when the temple took her, that she barely remembered her own family anymore, that she had a scar on her thumb from a dog she'd loved and could no longer picture, that she had taught herself to read from the temple's ledgers because those were the only books they'd let her near, that she thought she might have wanted to be a person who fixed things — clocks, engines, anything with an inside you could open up and understand — if anyone had ever once asked her what she wanted to be.

Selene learned that Kael had grown eleven different kinds of plant on the fire escape of a series of apartments, because his mother moved them a lot and a fire escape was the one thing that always came with the new place, that most of the plants had died and a stubborn few hadn't, that he had liked the not-dying ones best precisely because they were stubborn, that he had once wanted to be — he laughed, telling her, the garnet eyes going almost warm — a person who grew things on purpose. A farmer. A gardener. Something with dirt in it and a season to it and a reason to come back tomorrow.

"You wanted to grow things," Selene said. "And you ended up in the one place nothing grows."

"And you wanted to open things up and see how they worked," Kael said, "and they made you the thing everyone else opens up." He shook his head. "We're a matched set. Two people who wanted the exact opposite of what they got."

"Is that why you think we—" Selene stopped. Started again, braver. "Is that all this is? Two lonely people at the bottom of the world reaching for the nearest warm thing? Because if it is, I'd rather know. I've been used as a convenient thing my whole life. I'd rather be lonely honestly than be a warm object dishonestly."

Kael was quiet for a moment. She was learning that his quiet wasn't distance — it was the opposite. It was him taking her seriously enough to actually think.

"No," he said finally. "I don't think that's what it is. And here's how I know, and it's going to sound strange." He picked up a flat grey stone, turned it over, didn't throw it. "The fruit gives me this hunger, right? And the hunger wants things. It wants the throne. It wants power. It wants to stop being afraid by making everything else afraid instead. And when I'm near you, the hunger goes quiet — I told you that. But it's more than quiet. When I think about you, I don't *want* anything. That's the thing. I'm not reaching. I'm just — glad you exist, in a way that doesn't ask you for anything back. The hunger is all reaching. This is the opposite of reaching. If you were a warm thing I was grabbing at, the hunger would love you. It'd cheer me on. Instead it goes silent, because you're the one thing in this whole grey country it can't turn into an appetite." He set the stone down. "So no. I don't think you're the nearest warm thing. I think you might be the only thing that's ever made me stop being hungry. That's not the same. That's almost the reverse."

Selene looked at him across the small careful distance between them, and did not close it, not yet, and thought that no one had ever in her life said anything to her that she believed as completely as she believed that.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. I believe you." A breath. "For the record — the reason I keep coming back down a hole in the floor to sit by a dead river with a boy who's slowly turning into something else — it's not because you're warm either. It's because you ask me questions and wait for the answers. Do you have any idea how much of my life I have spent being talked *at*? Being pointed? You're the first person who ever treated the inside of my head like it had something worth waiting for." Her voice caught. "You want to know how to make me love you? That's it. That's the whole secret. You already did it, the first night, before you even knew my name. You just waited."

Neither of them moved to cross the distance. They just sat with it, in the quiet, on the grey shore, and let the wanting build slow and unspoken between them, because they both understood without saying it that the wanting was the good part, the part that was theirs, the part no throne had assigned and no prophecy had foreseen — and that once you spent a thing like that, you couldn't get it back unspent, and neither of them was in any hurry at all.

And he learned the other thing his father had warned him about, learned it slowly and precisely: that every time he used the power the fruit had given him, it cost him.

When he called the grey roads to fold themselves and bring Selene safely down to the shore, a little more of the taste of bread left him. When he walked a lost, wandering shade home to its rest, when he read the truth off a living heart the way he'd read the false promise off Hercules — small mercies, small kindnesses, each one so *obviously* good — he'd feel, afterward, some specific human thing quietly detach and drift away, like a leaf coming off a tree in the last week of autumn. The memory of what cold water felt like on a hot day. The exact sound of his mother's laugh. The way it had felt, once, to be afraid of the dark, back before he was made of it.

"You're doing it again," Selene said, one night. They were sitting on the shore, closer now — a hand's width apart, not an arm's — and she'd caught the small blankness that crossed his face after a mercy, the little absence. "You just gave something away. What was it this time?"

"I don't know," Kael admitted. "That's the horror of it. I only find out something's gone by the shape of the hole it leaves. Something about my mother, I think. There's a warmth where she used to be and I can't get to what made it warm." He looked down at his own hands. "My father warned me. Eyes first, then appetite, then loyalty. But he didn't tell me it does it through *kindness*. Every good thing I do down here spends a little more of the mortal in me. It's the cruelest design I've ever heard of. The fruit doesn't make you evil, Selene. It makes you *pay* for being good — in the one currency you can't ever earn back — until being good costs so much that one day you just decide, quietly, reasonably, to stop."

"Then stop using it," Selene said. "Around me, I mean. Let me find my own way down here. I'd rather climb than watch you lose your mother a piece at a time."

"Then I'd lose the reason I have to stay mortal at all." He said it simply, and didn't look at her when he said it, and Selene felt the words go straight through her sternum. "You're the ledger, Selene. You're the thing I'm keeping the human parts *for*. If I stop spending them to see you, I don't know what I'm saving them for anymore — and the hunger has a very good answer ready for that question, and I don't like the answer, and I don't want to hear myself agree with it."

The river did not ripple. Neither of them looked at it.

"That's not fair," Selene said quietly. "To put that on me. I'm not a reason to be good. I can't be your whole reason. That's — that's exactly how the temple used me, don't you see? I was the reason the god's will got done. I was the *instrument*. And there was no me left underneath it, none at all, I was just the thing other people's purposes moved through." Her voice was tight. "Don't make me your instrument too. Even a kind one. *Especially* a kind one. I've been a kind instrument. It's still an instrument."

Kael turned to look at her then. Really look. And Selene watched him take the correction all the way in — watched him hear it the way he'd heard her first question, head bent, absolutely still, the whole of him listening.

"You're right," he said. "I'm sorry. That was the fruit talking, or the loneliness — I can't always tell them apart anymore. Let me try it again." He was quiet a moment, working it out honestly, in front of her, which she was coming to understand was the thing he did that no one else in her life had ever done. "I don't want to be good *for* you. I want to be good — and I want you to *see* it. Because I've never had a witness. My whole life I was a boy nobody claimed, doing a job nobody watched. You're not my reason. You're just the first person who ever *looked*. There's a difference. You taught me there's a difference, the first night, when you asked me what I wanted instead of what I was."

"There's a difference," Selene agreed. Her voice was not steady.

They were very close now. Close enough that she could see the garnet of his eyes had darkened another shade since last week. Close enough that his breath — cold, the underworld's cold, and under it that terrible pomegranate sweetness — moved the loose hair at her temple.

"Selene," Kael said. "I need to tell you something, and I need to say it now, while I still remember that it's true, because I can feel it getting *expensive* to remember true things." He said it plainly, no performance in it at all. "When I'm with you, I stop being able to hear the hunger. That's the only hour in this whole grey country when it's quiet in my head. And I don't know if that's love, because I ate the fruit before I ever really learned what love was supposed to cost, so I've got nothing to measure it against. But I know it's the truest thing left in me. And I know it's the one thing the prophecy never accounted for. The god said I'd rise, and I'd be fed on the dark fruit, and I'd do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it. It never said one single word about a girl on a grey shore asking me what I wanted." His jaw tightened. "I think you might be the only thing in three thousand years that the wheel didn't see coming."

Selene closed the last of the distance between them and kissed him.

It was not gentle and it was not wise. It tasted of iron and cold and pomegranate, of the underworld and everything it had cost him, and she felt him go still against her the way he went still when he was listening with his whole body — and then his cold hand came up to the side of her face, careful, like she was the warmest thing he'd touched since he died into this. And for one moment, on the shore of a river that had never once moved in three thousand years, the pewter water broke into a thousand rings.

They both felt it. They pulled apart just far enough to look.

The river was moving. The unmovable river. The still, dead water of the underworld was rippling out from the shore where they sat — ring after ring after ring, spreading, as if something long asleep in the deep of the world had felt, for the first time in three thousand years, a reason to stir.

"That's not supposed to happen," Kael breathed.

Far below them, deeper than the deep, in the dark below even the dark — where a father-god had been chained for three thousand years — something that had been waiting longer than patience had a name opened one eye.

### Chapter Eight: What the Hound Was Promised

Hercules remembered his mother, which was more than most of the gods could say about anything mortal.

It was the oldest thing he had, that memory, older than the city, older than the war, worn smooth as a river stone from three and a half thousand years of handling. She had been a mortal woman in a mortal village, and she had been afraid — of the god who had come to her, of the child the god had left her with, of the neighbors who whispered, of the future she could not see the shape of. But she had held him anyway. That was the part he kept. She had been terrified of him, of what he was, of the strength that showed up in his baby hands before he could walk — and she had held him anyway, every night, and sung to him in a cracked frightened voice, and told him that being made half of something greater than yourself was not a curse but a debt. *You were given a gift, my love. Gifts are meant to be paid back. Serve well, and one day they'll take you up, and you'll never have to be afraid again.*

She had died the way mortals died, which is to say she had simply stopped, one ordinary year, while he stayed exactly as he was. And Hercules had taken the one thing she'd given him — *serve well, and one day they'll take you up* — and he had built his entire endless life on it, brick by brick, century by century, because it was the last warm thing he had and he could not bear for it to have been wrong.

That was the secret architecture of the hound. Not ambition. Not cruelty. Grief. He served the throne so faithfully for so long because his dead mortal mother had told him it would earn him a place where he would never have to be afraid again, and to stop believing that would be to admit that the last thing she ever gave him was a beautiful lie — and he would rather serve a tyrant for four thousand years than let his mother have died believing something false about the world she'd left her son in.

Jupiter had understood this about him, of course. Jupiter understood levers. He had never once had to threaten Hercules or chain him or coerce him. He had only ever had to say *when you have served enough* in exactly the cadence of a promise, and watch the demigod's face do the thing it did — the hope, the hunger, the small boy still listening to a cracked voice sing in the dark — and know that he owned him completely, for free, forever, on the strength of a dead woman's love.

Hercules had never let himself see it that way. He saw it now. He'd started seeing it on the grey shore, when a garnet-eyed boy said *you reek of a promise nobody's ever going to keep*, and he had not been able to stop seeing it since, and it was the loneliest thing that had ever happened to him, lonelier than the mother, lonelier than the war. Because it meant that the warmest thing he owned might have been a leash the whole time.

He was thinking about her — about the cracked voice, about *never have to be afraid again* — when he came back up the crack that night from another hunt he had never actually gone on.

---

Hercules didn't sleep. Demigods didn't, not really. But he had a room in the tower where he went to not-sleep, and Juno was sitting in it, in the dark, when he came in.

"You haven't found him," the Queen of Heaven said.

She was in his single chair, in the dark, and she hadn't turned on any lights, and Hercules understood at once that this was a conversation he'd been summoned to without being told — which was the only kind of conversation Juno ever had.

"No," he said.

"That's strange." She turned something over in her long fingers. He thought it was a coin at first; then he saw it was a pomegranate seed, dried black, the kind the temple kept sealed in its deepest vault as evidence of what a marking looked like. "Because I found him. It took me one afternoon. He's on the grey shore of the fifth river most nights. And two or three of those nights, he isn't alone." She let that sit in the dark. "You've known where he is for weeks, Hercules. You've been coming back up that crack in the floor and looking my husband in the eye and telling him the trail's gone cold. You. The one who has never lied to the throne in three and a half thousand years." She finally looked up at him. "I don't understand it. And I don't like things I don't understand standing this close to my husband's throne. So explain it to me. Slowly. And know that I already suspect the answer — so if you lie to me too, I'll know, and then you'll have lied to both of us, and there won't be a fire big enough in all the world to burn *that* off you."

The word landed exactly where she had aimed it. *Fire.*

She knew about the pyre. Of course she knew. Juno read everything.

Hercules sat down heavily on the edge of the bed he never used, and for the first time in longer than most nations had existed, he told the whole truth to someone who ruled him.

"He offered to stop the wheel," he said. "The boy. He doesn't want the throne. I stood on that shore ready to put him in the ground, and he held out his empty hands and told me he only wants to stop being hunted — and that that isn't the same as wanting the crown — and that everyone in the whole story wants him to believe it's the same." Hercules stared at his own scarred hands. "And I realized I'm the wheel, Juno. I'm the thing that keeps it turning. Every rival, every son, every patient enemy for three and a half thousand years — I'm the arm the throne sends so that it never has to change. I thought I was protecting the world. I've been protecting *him*. One man. And he promised me a fire three and a half thousand years ago, and every century he tells me *when*, and the boy — the boy could smell it on me. He said I reek of a promise nobody's ever going to keep." He looked up at her, and his voice was suddenly that of a much younger, much more breakable creature than he had been in a very long time. "So tell me it's real, Juno. The pyre. The ascension. Tell me the *when* is coming, and I'll go back down that crack tonight and end the boy and the girl both and never think of the shore again. You *run* this throne. You'd know. Is my fire real?"

Juno was quiet for a long moment in the dark.

And Hercules — who could read a battlefield in a single glance, who had won and lost more wars than the living had names for — watched the Queen of Heaven decide whether to lie to him. Watched her weigh it, coldly, the way she weighed everything: the cost of the truth against the cost of the lie. And he had his answer before she ever opened her mouth, because a promise that was real would not have needed weighing.

"No," Juno said.

She didn't soften it. That was, in its own bleak way, the kindest thing anyone had done for Hercules in three and a half thousand years.

"There is no pyre coming, Hercules. There was never going to be one. My husband cannot make a god — only the wheel of succession makes gods, and he *broke* that wheel on purpose, the day he chained his own father, precisely so that no one new could ever rise to threaten him again. He promised you a fire in the same century that he made that fire structurally impossible. You have served three and a half thousand years for a door he welded shut before you were even born." She set the dried seed down on the little table between them, gently, like she was laying down a body. "I'm telling you this for three reasons. Because I am done cleaning up my husband's messes. Because a hound that finally understands its own leash is the most useful thing in this whole city. And because — and you'll believe this last one least of all — because you deserved to hear the truth from *someone*, once, before the end. And my husband was never going to be the someone."

The tower was very quiet. Far below, the city glittered, small and owned.

"Then what do I do," Hercules said. It was barely a sound at all.

"You choose," said Juno. "For the first time in your entire existence. The same as the boy. The same as the girl." She rose, and smoothed her dress, and at the door she paused, one hand on the frame. "The wheel my husband broke to save himself — I have spent six thousand years wondering what would happen if it turned again. Whether anything actually changes, or whether we just grind out a new king from the same old grain and start the whole thing over. I don't know the answer. But I've decided that I would rather find out than spend one more century watching that man mistake fear for love and call it *ruling*." She opened the door onto the cold bright hall. "The boy doesn't want the throne, you say. Good. That might be the only thing that makes any of this worth doing. Go back down your crack, Hercules. Tell the boy the Queen of Heaven is tired of her husband. And tell him to *hurry* — because I lied to my husband tonight, too, to buy you this conversation. And Jupiter can smell a lie in his own house the way that boy can smell one on you."

### Chapter Nine: A Small Cruelty, Witnessed

Selene almost didn't go to the tower the day the summons came. But an Oracle who refused Jupiter was an Oracle who confessed, and so she went. And she was glad she went — glad was a strange word for it, but she never found a better one — because it was in the tower that day that she stopped, finally and forever, being able to tell herself the throne was worth protecting.

There was a mortal man in Jupiter's audience hall when she arrived.

He'd found his way up. A petitioner — one of the rare ones who stumbled through the thin place in the world and remembered enough to come asking. He had a sick child, Selene gathered, listening from the side of the room where the Oracle was made to stand like a piece of furniture that happened to have eyes. His daughter was dying, and he had prayed, and prayed, and somehow the praying had worn a hole clear through the world and dropped him out here, at the actual foot of the actual throne, begging the actual god.

Jupiter was in a wonderful mood. That was the part Selene would never be able to forget. He was *charmed*.

"You walked through the seam," Jupiter said, delighted, leaning forward on the throne. "Do you have any idea how rare that is, little man? To want a thing so badly that the *world itself thins* for you? I could count on one hand the mortals who've managed it this century." He beamed down at the trembling father. "Your daughter. Yes. I know the one. Fever of the lung, isn't it — she has, oh, perhaps a week left. So. Here is what I'll do." He held up a single finger, and the whole hall leaned in to watch it. "I'll save her. Truly. One word from me and the fever breaks tonight and she lives to be ninety years old. It's a small thing. It's well within my power. It costs me *nothing*." His smile widened, warm and terrible. "And all I ask in return — because everything has a cost, that is the one honest thing about power — is that you tell me, right now, in front of my whole court, that you love me more than you love her. Say it. And mean it. And she lives."

The hall went silent.

The father's face did a thing that Selene would see behind her own eyes for the rest of her life. He understood the trap. He understood there was no version of this that didn't break him. If he said it, he would carry the lie until he died — and worse, some part of it might come *true*, because words spoken at the foot of a god have weight and don't always let you take them back. And if he refused, his daughter died for his pride, and he would carry *that*, instead, forever.

"There's no answer that saves us both," the father whispered.

"No," Jupiter agreed. Warmly. Kindly, almost. "There isn't. There never is. That's the lesson, little man — not the daughter; the daughter is just the tuition you're paying for it. The lesson is that I am the only thing in your entire world with no cost above me, and that everything you love is a lever I can reach out and pull, and the sooner every single mortal in my city understands *that*, all the way down in the marrow, the more smoothly we all get along." He settled back into the throne. "Take your time deciding. She has a week. I have forever."

And Selene, standing at the side of the hall like a piece of furniture with eyes, understood the whole architecture of the throne in one sick, dropping lurch.

It wasn't that Jupiter was cruel. Cruelty was too small a word — too hot, too personal. It was that Jupiter had ruled so long that he no longer experienced other people's love as anything but *inventory*. A stock of levers, to be pulled or not pulled, priced against his own comfort. He wasn't torturing the man for fun. He genuinely, in the deepest chamber of his three-thousand-year-old heart, could not *conceive* of a reason not to. There was no one left in his world who wasn't a lever. He'd made sure of that. He'd spent three thousand years making sure of that. And that — Selene understood, staring at the shattered father — *that* was what the throne did to whoever sat in it long enough.

*That's the wheel,* she thought. *That's what Kael would become. Not because he's cruel. Because that is what the chair does to whoever sits in it for three thousand years. The son does to the throne what the throne did to the one before — and then the throne does to the son what it did to the father. It's not a curse on a bloodline. It's a curse on the chair.*

She did not remember deciding to speak. She only knew that she was, suddenly, speaking — in the Oracle's voice, the god-voice, the one voice in that hall that even Jupiter had to allow to finish.

"The god has a word," Selene said.

The hall turned. Jupiter's warm eyes sharpened with interest, because a spontaneous transmission was rare, and rare things were valuable.

She had no transmission. She was about to gamble her life on a single bet: that no one in that room could tell the difference between the god speaking through her, and Selene, at last, speaking for herself.

"The god says," she went on, and her voice rang out with the full borrowed weight she had spent two years learning to counterfeit — the weight that even a god believed — "*the child lives, and no price is named. For a king who charges for his mercy has already sold the only thing that ever made him worth the kneeling.*"

The silence was total.

She had just publicly told Jupiter — in the borrowed authority of the very god-voice that legitimized his entire throne — that charging for mercy made him worthless. In front of his whole court. She waited to die.

Jupiter looked at her for a long, long moment.

And then he laughed. That warm, delighted laugh, and he clapped his hands together once, and the sound of it cracked through the hall like the lightning behind his eyes. "The *god* scolds me!" he cried, sounding genuinely charmed. "Even the *god* thinks I overplay it! Fine. Fine! The child lives, little man — no charge — go home, the fever's already breaking, tell no one, and remember it was a *whim*. Remember that mercy in my house is *weather*, and not *law*." He waved the father out, still chuckling, and the man fled with his daughter's life and, somehow, his own soul both intact, and Selene's knees nearly gave out beneath her.

But as the hall emptied, Jupiter beckoned her close. And when she came, he took her cold hands in his warm ones, and he was still smiling, and his eyes had gone the flat grey of a storm that has finally decided to make landfall.

"That was very good, Selene," he murmured, for her ears alone. "Very brave. Very clever. I almost believed the god was truly in you just then." His grip tightened by one single degree — one degree of frost, one degree of the power that had chained a father in the dark below the dark. "Here is the thing about being the door the gods speak through, little Oracle. A door doesn't get *opinions*. The moment a door starts deciding which way it wants to swing — that is the moment it stops being a door. And a door that won't stay a door is just wood. And you know what we do with wood." He lifted her frozen hands to his lips and kissed them, gently, like a beloved uncle. "Find me my nephew. You've stalled long enough. Hercules has gone strange, and you have gone strange, and I am beginning to feel — I, who feel almost nothing anymore — a very old, very cold thing that I have not felt since my brother first learned how to wait. Find the boy. Point. Or I will begin pulling the levers I have on *you*. And I promise you, child — I have already found exactly where they are."

He released her.

Selene walked out of the tower on legs that did not feel like hers, out into the ordinary golden evening of a city that did not know it was owned, and understood that she had, at last, run out of room to stand between the two brothers. The Queen had warned her: *an Oracle who chose is an Oracle remembered as a traitor.*

She was going to choose anyway.

She had already chosen, she realized. Weeks ago. On a grey shore, when a dying boy asked her what she wanted, and then waited to hear the answer.

Now she just had to survive it.

### Chapter Ten: The Merciful Lie

Marcus knew something was wrong before Selene did.

That was the thing about the steward: he was frightened, and frightened people watch closely. He had spent two years learning the exact rhythms of the asset he was charged with keeping, and the rhythms had changed. Selene slept less. She flinched at the summons. She had begun asking, in careful roundabout ways, about the temple's contingencies — what happened to an Oracle who could no longer serve, whether the reserve girl could be sent home instead of kept, whether there was any version of the temple that let a door become just a person again. There wasn't. Marcus told her there wasn't, and watched her face when he did, and did not like what he saw there, because what he saw there was a girl doing arithmetic that ended somewhere he couldn't follow.

"Selene," he said to her, two days before the end, in the cold stone hall outside the rite-chamber. "I have kept you alive for two years. Do you understand that? Not the temple. Me. I have fed you and covered for you and lied about your headaches and stood between you and men who would have used you up in a month. I am not clever, and I am not brave, and I am frightened of you, and I have done it anyway, because it is my job and because you were eight years old when they brought you and somebody ought to have kept the promise the temple made your family and broke." He twisted his hands, the way he did. "So I am asking you, as the only person in this building who has ever wanted anything for you that wasn't *use* — whatever you are doing, stop. Whatever you are protecting, let it go. Point where they tell you to point. Because I cannot keep you alive from a mistake this size, and I have watched three girls before you go into the ground, and I do not think my heart will do a fourth."

Selene looked at him — this anxious, ordinary, decent man, who had loved her the only clumsy way the temple allowed, from behind the glass of his own fear — and understood that he was right, and that she was going to do it anyway, and that she could not tell him, because telling him would make him a place the throne could push.

"You've been kind to me, Marcus," she said. "Truly. And I'm going to do something soon that's going to make it look like I threw your kindness away. I want you to know now, before it happens, that I didn't. I'm keeping it. I'm just — I've found something worth more than staying alive on the terms they're offering. That's all. I found out the terms were never going to change, and I found something that made me stop being willing to pay them."

"Selene—"

"Look after Iris," Selene said. "When it's done. Whatever *done* turns out to mean. Promise me you'll look after Iris, and get her out if you can, and don't let them make her the next door. Promise me that, and I'll go into the rite tomorrow and do exactly what I have to do, and you won't have to understand it."

And Marcus, who did not understand, and who was frightened, and who was decent, promised. Because it was the only thing left he could give her.

She held onto that, the next day, kneeling barefoot in front of the throne. Not the god-voice, not the temple, not the throne. A frightened steward's promise about an eleven-year-old with a gap in her teeth. It turned out that was the thing worth lying to a god for. Not the cosmic stuff. The small stuff. The stuff no prophecy ever bothered to mention.

---

It came down, in the end, to a single sentence.

Jupiter's patience ran out eight days later. He summoned the whole apparatus of the throne into the audience hall — Juno cold at his side, Hercules stone-faced in the enforcer's place by the wall, the temple stewards clustered and nervous, Marcus wringing his hands raw — and Selene, barefoot on the cold floor, because the god-voice does not come to the well-dressed, brought before the throne to do at last the single thing an Oracle exists to do.

"Enough," Jupiter said. There was no warmth in it now. None at all. Just three thousand years of a fear he could no longer be bothered to disguise as charm. "The god showed you the boy. So you will stand before this court, Oracle, and you will speak the true prophecy, in the god's own voice, and you will tell me exactly where my nephew stands and what he intends. And Hercules will end it tonight. No more dreams. No more walks to clear your head. No more scolding me about mercy." He leaned forward, and the glass walls of the hall fogged with cold. "Speak. And know that I will *feel* it if you lie. I have heard the true god-voice for three thousand years. I know its weight in my bones. So speak true — or do not speak at all. But if you say nothing, I'll know that too. I'll know why. And I will pull every lever I have on you, one by one, until there is nothing left of the Oracle to point with."

They brought the basin. Black water, carried up from the temple in a vessel of old stone. Selene knelt at its edge, on the cold floor, and put her hands into the water.

And for the first time in her entire life, she did not listen for the god.

She listened for herself.

Here was the terrible arithmetic of it, the choice the whole story had been sharpening toward, laid out cold and clear in the black water:

She could speak the true prophecy. *The son has risen, fed on the dark fruit, and will do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it.* It was true. She had heard it from the god's own mouth. And if she spoke it true, in the god-voice that Jupiter could not doubt, she would confirm Kael as the threat, and Hercules — whatever he'd promised on the shore — would be commanded, openly, by a god he could not yet openly defy. And the hunt would end on the grey shore, with the boy she loved put into the ground.

Or she could lie. A merciful lie, in the god-voice, and gamble everything on the hope that even Jupiter's three-thousand-year-old ear could not tell the difference between the god speaking through her and Selene, at last, fully and finally becoming a liar for love.

She thought of the father in this same hall. *There's no answer that saves us both.*

She thought of Kael on the shore, spending his mother's laugh a piece at a time just to sit beside her.

She thought of Juno: *point where you're told to point.*

And Selene — the fourth Oracle in twelve years, the door that had decided which way it wanted to swing — opened her mouth and did the one thing she had been made never, ever to do.

"The god speaks," she said, and her voice rang with the borrowed weight she had spent two years learning to counterfeit, the weight that even a god believed. "Hear the god. *The son of the deep is a false alarm.*"

The hall went utterly, breathlessly still. Jupiter's eyes narrowed.

"*The prophecy the throne fears has already broken of its own weight,*" Selene went on, and the lie poured out of her clean and cold and perfect, dressed head to foot in the god's own robes. "*The boy ate the fruit, yes — but the fruit did not take. He is unmarked in every way that matters. He has no wish for the throne, no army, no reach, no hunger; he is a frightened mortal lost in a dead man's country. And if the throne strikes him down, it will accomplish nothing but to prove the deep worthy of all the fear the throne has wasted on it. Leave the boy to the dark. He will die there — mortal, and forgotten — and the wheel the throne fears will not turn, because there is no hand upon it. This the god swears: strike, and you make the prophecy true by the striking. Wait, and it dies of its own smallness. The choice is the throne's alone. The god has spoken.*"

She pulled her hands from the water.

The silence stretched to the very edge of breaking. Jupiter stared at her, and Selene made herself hold his eyes, made herself be a door and not a girl, made herself *empty* — and she prayed, to no god, to the empty air, to the memory of a river that had learned to ripple, that three thousand years of listening had not taught the tyrant to hear the one thing she could not fully hide.

The love. It was in the lie. She could feel it in there — a warmth the true god-voice didn't have — and if Jupiter knew the god's weight the way he claimed, he would feel the wrongness of that warmth like a cold hand on the back of his neck.

"Husband," Juno said quietly, into the silence.

The Queen's voice was flat, and bored, and absolutely certain. "The god has never once lied to us in three thousand years. Are you truly going to burn the entire apparatus of your own temple to the ground — admit, in front of your whole court, that you no longer trust the very oracle that legitimizes your throne — over a frightened boy the god itself has just called *small*?" She examined her fingernails. "Strike, and you make it true. The god said so, plainly. I have cleaned up enough of your instincts over six thousand years, husband, to know this one for what it is. Let the boy rot. Save the drama for something that's actually coming."

It was, Selene understood, a second lie. Laid over her own like a second coat of paint. From the one person in that room whose word Jupiter half-trusted and wholly feared.

Juno had chosen. The Queen of Heaven had lied to her husband's face to save the boy who didn't want the throne. *I'd rather find out than spend one more century watching that man mistake fear for love.*

Jupiter looked from his Oracle to his Queen and back again, and the storm behind his eyes circled, and circled, and did not — quite — make landfall.

"Leave the boy," he said at last. Slowly. Tasting every word for poison, and finding — because two women had hidden the poison very, very well — none. "For now. But Hercules watches that crack in the floor. And the first sign — the very first — that the god was wrong, or the god was *lied to*—" his eyes flicked to Selene, and lingered there, one degree of frost "—we revisit all of this. Every part of it. Including who spoke, and in whose voice." He rose from the throne. "You may go, Oracle. You have served the throne today. Sleep well."

Selene walked out of the hall on her own legs this time — barely — and did not let herself shake until the elevator doors had slid shut. And then she shook so hard she had to hold onto the rail with both hands.

She had done it. She had stood in front of the tyrant who chained a god in the dark, and she had lied to save the boy she loved, and she had lived.

But she had also, she understood, dressed the god's own mouth in a lie. The god who did not forget. The god whose voice was the one true thing the throne could not counterfeit. And there are older laws than Jupiter's, and older prices, and somewhere very far below — in the deep beneath the deep — the thing that had opened one eye at the sight of a rippling river now opened the other.

Because a false word, spoken in the true god-voice, is exactly the kind of crack that a patient thing can climb.

### Chapter Eleven: What Stirs Beneath the Deep

Kael felt it before Verus told him.

He woke — insofar as he still slept — with the taste of iron flooding his mouth and the absolute certain knowledge that something in the underworld had *shifted*. Some vast, slow weight redistributing itself, far below the deepest road he had ever walked.

He found his father standing at the edge of the fifth river, in the exact place where the water had first learned to ripple. And Pluto's ancient face was turned down toward the deep with an expression Kael had never seen on it before.

Fear. His father. The god of the dead. Afraid.

"You feel it," Pluto said, without turning around.

"What is it?"

"Him." The single word carried three thousand years in it. "Saturn. Your grandfather. The one Jupiter chained below the dark — so deep that even I cannot go there. So deep that the chaining itself was the last cruelty a son could do a father. Worse than death. Because death would have freed him, and Jupiter would not grant him even that." Pluto's garnet eyes, so like Kael's own, stared down into the pewter water. "He has been stirring ever since the river learned to move. Something reached the deep that hadn't reached it in three thousand years — a word spoken in the true god-voice that was *not true*. It made a crack. And a patient thing, in a deep place, will always, always climb a crack." He finally turned to look at his son. "Your Oracle lied to the throne to save you. I know. I felt the shape of it move through the water. It was the bravest thing I have watched a mortal do in an age. And it may have doomed us all — because the god-voice is a *key*, Kael, and she turned it in a lock that has been holding your grandfather down for three thousand years."

Kael went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the underworld.

"What happens if he gets out."

"I don't know." Pluto said it heavily. "No one does. No one alive remembers Saturn ruling — I was young when Jupiter threw him down; younger than you can imagine, though I hide it well. But I remember this: Saturn was not chained for being weak. He was chained because he could not be beaten any other way. Because he had a hunger of his own — the oldest hunger, the one the fruit in your blood is only a small dark echo of. Saturn is the one who *taught* the wheel to turn, Kael. He ate his own children to keep his throne from ever passing on. Jupiter survived only because your great-grandmother hid him away. And now Saturn has spent three thousand years in the dark below the dark, with nothing to do at all but hate — and a crack has opened above him, and he is climbing." He put a hand on his son's shoulder. It was the first time the god had ever touched him. "The prophecy said you would do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it. Everyone assumed that meant Jupiter. But there is an *older* one before, my son. There is always an older one. And I think the god was never talking about you overthrowing your uncle at all. I think the god was talking about you being the hinge on which a much older, much heavier door finally swings open."

Far below, the deep exhaled.

The whole underworld shuddered. The fields of pale flowers screamed — a thin, papery, horrible sound, all of them at once. And up through the fifth river came a smell that made even Pluto flinch: not the sweet turned-earth of the fruit, but something older, and colder, the smell of time itself gone rancid, of a hunger that predated mercy, that predated the very idea that hunger might ever be asked to stop.

"Get your Oracle," Pluto said. And now there was command in it — the god of the dead, at last, fully awake. "Get the hound, if he'll come. And the Queen, if she truly means it. And get them all to the deep before Saturn reaches the roads. Because when he does — when he reaches the surface — there will be no throne left to fight over. He will eat Jupiter. And he will eat me. And he will eat you. And then he will eat every soul in this grey country and every mortal in that glittering city above it, and he will sit down on the whole wheel of the world, and the wheel will finally, truly stop turning — because there will be nothing left alive to grind." Pluto's garnet eyes burned. "You did not want the throne. Good. Because the only way to stop him now is *worse* than taking it. You will have to become enough of a king to chain a god — the very thing you swore you would never be — or you will have to watch everything that has ever lived come to an end. That is the choice, Kael. That was always going to be the choice. The fruit knew it before you did. That's why it was so patient with you."

## Part Three: The Wheel Turns

### Chapter Twelve: The Gathering of the Ones Who Chose

They met, the six of them, in the lobby with the crack in the floor.

It was the exact place where the whole story had started — where a boy on his knees had first felt the cold come up through the marble. There was a kind of rightness to it that none of them said out loud.

Kael. Selene. Hercules, who had come down the crack when Juno's word reached him and had not gone back up. Juno herself, who had walked out of the tower in broad daylight for the first time in a century and told her husband she was going to the temple to pray, and who now stood in the marble lobby in a plain grey coat, looking, for the first time Selene had ever seen, like a woman and not a queen. Pluto, risen from his grey country to the very threshold of the living world, as far up as an exiled god was permitted to come, the cold pouring off him. And Verus, silent by the door, keeping it as he had kept it for three thousand years.

"Let me be certain that I understand this," Juno said. "Before I commit treason in a form there is no walking back from. The lie the Oracle told to save the boy cracked the seal on Saturn. My husband's father. The one he chained below the dark. And that father is now climbing up through the deep — and if he reaches the surface, he ends not just the throne, but the world." She looked at Pluto. "And the only way to stop him is to put him back. Which requires a hand strong enough to chain a god. Which none of us here alone possesses. And which the boy could possess only by taking up the very power he swore he never would."

"Yes," Pluto said.

"So the prophecy was always going to come true," Selene said quietly.

She was standing close to Kael, and her hand had found his somewhere along the way, and neither of them had decided to do it. "One way or the other. Kael does to the throne what the throne did to the one before. Either he takes Jupiter's crown — or he chains Jupiter's father. And either way, he ends up the god who cast an elder down into the dark. The wheel turns, and it finds its new king. That's the trap. That's what the god was really saying, underneath the words."

"Not necessarily," said Hercules.

They all turned to look at him. The hound of the throne had said very little since coming down. Now he stepped into the center of the marble lobby, and there was something in his face that had not been there in three and a half thousand years — the face of a man who has discovered, very late, that he is allowed to think.

"I have spent my whole existence," Hercules said, "being the arm that keeps the wheel from turning, so that one man can stay on top of it forever. And Juno told me the truth: the wheel *can't* turn anymore, because Jupiter broke it on purpose the day he chained his father, precisely so that no new god could ever rise. He welded the succession shut. That's why my pyre never came — you can't make a new god in a world where the wheel's been welded." He turned to Kael. "But here is what I've been chewing on, boy. All these nights I couldn't sleep. Everyone in this room keeps saying the wheel does one thing, and only one thing: the son casts the father down, forever, into the dark. Saturn did it to his children — by eating them. Jupiter did it to Saturn — by chaining him below the dark. And the prophecy says you'll do it to Jupiter." He set his jaw. "But what if the wheel only grinds forever because *every single time, the son chose the darkest ending that was available to him?* Eat the children. Chain the father. Bury the old king so deep he can never climb back. What if the wheel isn't a curse on the chair at all — what if it's just three thousand years of every heir being too frightened to try the one thing that not one of them ever tried?"

"Which is what?" Kael asked. His voice was rough.

"*Mercy*," said Hercules. "The one thing none of you godlings has ever once extended to the one you overthrew. Saturn ate. Jupiter chained. If you win, Kael — and you do what they did — the wheel turns, and grinds, and finds its next king, and it never, ever stops. But if you win, and you *free* the one below instead of burying him deeper—" the hound's voice cracked "—then maybe *that's* the thing that stops it. Maybe the wheel doesn't stop when a kinder king climbs on top of it. Maybe it stops the very first time somebody climbs *off*."

The lobby was silent. The crack in the floor breathed cold.

"Saturn will eat us if we free him," Pluto said. But slowly. As if the certainty had gone out of the words even as he said them.

"Saturn has spent three thousand years in the dark below the dark, with nothing but his own hunger for company," Selene said, and they all turned to her — the Oracle, the door that had learned to speak for itself. "Because his own son could not imagine an ending other than the one that his own father had taught him. Don't you see? Everyone in this whole story is trapped inside the last cruelty they were shown. Jupiter can only see levers, because levers are all Saturn ever let him be. Kael can only see the throne or the ending, because the throne or the ending is all the fruit knows how to want." Her hand tightened in Kael's. "I lied to a god to save one boy. It cracked the world. And I have spent two years being the mouth that other people's cruelty spoke through, and I am *done* being the instrument of an ending I didn't choose. If we're going to turn the wheel — then let's turn it somewhere it has never been. Nobody in three thousand years has ever tried simply asking the monster in the dark what he *wants*." She looked at Kael, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "It worked on you."

Kael looked down at the girl holding his hand.

The first person who had ever asked him what he wanted and then waited for the answer. The crack the wheel never saw coming. The ledger he'd kept his mortal heart in. The reason — no. The *witness*.

"All right," he said. "Then here is the plan. And it's the worst plan anyone has ever made. And it is the only one that isn't just the wheel wearing a new face." He looked around the ruined lobby at the six who had chosen. "We go down. Past my father's country. Past the last road. Into the dark below the dark. And we don't go to chain Saturn deeper. And we don't go to kill him — because you cannot kill the thing that taught death itself to the world. We go down to *free* him. And the second we do, Jupiter is going to feel his father come loose, and he is going to come down that crack with three thousand years of lightning to shove him back into the dark — because a tyrant would rather burn the whole world down than admit the wheel can turn in a direction he can't control." He squeezed Selene's hand once. "So we're not really going down to fight Saturn. We are going down to *reach him first*. Before Jupiter can turn him into a weapon, or into a corpse. We are going to be the first heirs in the entire history of the wheel who reach the one below and offer him something other than a deeper hole."

"And if he simply eats us anyway?" Hercules asked.

Kael's garnet eyes were steady. "Then we'll have died trying the one thing that nobody ever tried — instead of surviving by doing the thing that everybody always does." He almost smiled. "My whole life, nobody ever claimed me. Turns out the one upside of that is I've got nothing left to protect but the choice itself. So let's go turn the wheel off its track."

Far above, in a tower of cold glass, Jupiter's warm eyes opened in the dark. And for the first time in three thousand years, the god who ruled the sky felt his own father move beneath the world.

### Chapter Thirteen: The Descent

There are no words for the roads below the last road.

Kael understood, going down them, why his father had never once spoken of the deep: because the deep did not permit speech. Only endurance. The grey country gave way to black. The black gave way to a dark that was not the absence of light but the presence of something that *ate* it — that leaned in close and drank the light off your skin and left you colder for it. And through all of it, the six of them walked, small figures descending a stair that went down and down and down into the root of everything, because there was no other direction left in all the world to go.

The fruit-hunger in Kael grew louder the deeper they went.

He'd expected that. He had dreaded it. And it was worse than he'd dreaded, because down here, this close to the oldest hunger of all, the small dark echo of it in his own blood strained toward its source like a compass needle dragged toward true north. *Take it,* the hunger sang, in his own reasonable voice. *Take the power. Take the throne. Chain the old thing back down and rule the wheel and never be hungry or hunted again, not ever. It's right there. It's yours. It's justice, and it's yours, and all you have to do is reach.* And it was so hard — so exactly, precisely hard, just as his father had warned him — to tell it apart from the real thing.

"Kael." Selene's hand on his arm, in the eating dark. She'd felt him slowing, felt the pull dragging at him. "Where are you right now?"

"On the stair," he managed. "With you. I think. The hunger's very loud down here."

"Then ask me a question," Selene said. "Any question. A real one. And wait for the answer. That's the one thing it can't do — the hunger only ever tells you what you already want to hear. It can't ask, and it can't wait. So ask me something you don't already know the answer to. And hold on to me while I say it."

Kael held onto her voice in the dark like a rope thrown to a drowning man.

"When this is over," he said, "if there's an over — what do *you* want, Selene? Not what the temple wants. Not what saves the world. You. I've been so busy being everyone's prophecy that I never once asked you the same question you asked me on the shore." He turned toward her in the dark. "What do you want? Really. When all of this is done."

And Selene — who had been chosen and never asked, who had spent two years being a door, who had lied to a god and cracked the world open for the boy beside her — was quiet for a long moment, on the stair below the world.

"I want a small life," she said. And it came out shaking, and true. "That's the secret I've never said out loud to anyone. Everyone keeps handing me *cosmic* things. Be the Oracle. Save the boy. Turn the wheel. And all I have ever actually wanted, underneath all of it, is to be no one's instrument. To have a morning that belongs to me. To choose one small, ordinary thing, and have it stay chosen." Her voice broke. "I want to *want* something, and have it be *mine*, and I have never once in my whole life had that. And the closest I've ever come was a grey shore, where a dying boy asked me what I wanted, and then actually waited to hear it." She found his face in the dark with her cold, warm, living hand. "I want you, Kael. That's the smallest and the largest true thing I've got. Not the son of Hades. Not the prophecy. Not the king you're so afraid of becoming. The boy on the shore who went completely still when I talked. I want him to survive this. And I want a small life with him afterward. And I know that's not allowed in a story this size — and I want it anyway."

The hunger in Kael's blood went, for a moment, completely silent.

Because it could not follow her there. It had no answer for *a small life*. It knew appetite, and thrones, and endings — but it did not know, had never known, how to want a morning that belonged to you and no one else. Selene had just said aloud the one thing in all the world that the oldest hunger could not translate. And in the silence that it left behind, Kael heard his own heart — the mortal one, the one he'd been spending a piece at a time on a grey shore — still beating.

But it wasn't only Kael the deep worked on. It worked on all of them, each in the language of their own oldest fear, and somewhere on the endless stair the plan very nearly came apart.

It was Jupiter who broke first — Jupiter, climbing down at the back of them, hands bound in a cord of Pluto's making that would not have held him for a heartbeat if he'd truly wanted free, and everyone knew it, which was the point. He had come because Juno made him and because the alternative was being left alone in a tower with the knowledge of what was climbing toward it. But the deeper they went, the closer they came to his father, the more the three-thousand-year-old terror wound itself back up in him, until he stopped on the stair and would not move.

"This is madness," Jupiter said, and his voice had gone high and strange. "You're going to free him. You're actually going to free him. Do you know what he *is*? None of you were there. I was there. I was a child hidden in a cave because my own father would have swallowed me whole, and you want to walk down and take the chains off him and *ask him nicely*—" He rounded on Pluto. "Brother. You of all of them. He never even knew your name. He'd have eaten you first because you were smallest. And you'd help these children unchain him?"

"Yes," Pluto said. "Because I have spent three thousand years being what you made me — buried, and patient, and full of hate — and I have just watched my son do in one hour what neither of us managed in an age. He asked. He waited. And it turns out the monster at the bottom of everything was only ever another frightened thing that no one ever asked." His garnet eyes held his brother's. "You could climb off this stair right now, Jupiter, and go back up, and be the last king of a stopped world. Or you could come down with us and find out that the thing you've been running from your whole life is as tired as you are. I can't make you. Nobody down here can make anybody do anything anymore. That's rather the point."

And Selene, watching the two ancient brothers on the stair in the eating dark, understood that this — not Saturn, not the lightning still to come — was the real hinge of it. Because Jupiter was the wheel's truest believer. Kael had the fruit whispering in him, but Jupiter *was* the fruit's whole argument made flesh: three thousand years of proof that the only way to survive the wheel was to keep it turning. If he could not be moved, even a little, even just enough to come down the stairs, then the deep would win, and Saturn would be a weapon, and the whole thing would end the way it always ended.

"My lord," she said — the Oracle, one last time, but in her own voice, no borrowed weight at all. "You asked me, once, why the god would show me a thing that wasn't meant for the throne. I didn't have an answer then. I do now. The god showed me the boy because the god is tired too. Three thousand years of being a mouth for other people's fear — do you think the god *likes* being the thing that legitimizes a wheel? You built a whole religion to make your terror sound like order. And the one true thing in all of it, the god-voice, the thing you couldn't fake — it spent its one prophecy in an age trying to tell you the wheel could stop. *He will do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it.* You heard a death sentence. But there's another way to read it, my lord, and it took me until right now to hear it. What if it just meant: *the next one gets to choose what he does with the one who came before.* That's all. Not a curse. A door. The god wasn't warning you. The god was *begging* you. And you've spent three thousand years too frightened to hear the difference."

The deep was silent. Even the dark seemed to lean in.

Jupiter looked at the seventeen-year-old girl who had lied to his face and saved his enemy and cracked open the world, and something in his ancient, terrified expression flickered — not surrender, not yet, but the first hairline crack of a question in a wall that had held for three thousand years.

"I don't know how to stop being afraid," he said. It was, again, the most honest thing he'd ever said. "I wouldn't know where to begin."

"Neither did any of us," said Kael, from lower down the stair. "You begin by going down instead of up. That's it. That's the whole first step. The rest we figure out at the bottom."

And Jupiter — the tyrant, the sky-king, the wheel's last believer — did not turn back up the stair.

He came down.

"Then let's go free a god," Kael said, "so we can go and have a small life. In that order. Come on." He turned back to the dark, and his voice was his own again, all the way through. "I can hear the bottom now. We're almost there."

They reached the floor of the deep below the dark.

And Saturn was waiting.

### Chapter Fourteen: The One Below

He was not what any of them had expected. And he was worse. And he was — this was the thing that undid Kael, standing at the bottom of everything — he was so *tired*.

Saturn was vast. That was the first fact of him, the one the eye reported before the mind could soften it into anything bearable: he filled the deep the way a held breath fills a chest, a presence more than a shape, immense and old and folded in on himself in the dark. The chains that Jupiter had made were everywhere — threaded through him, and through the black rock, and through the very idea of *down* itself. Three thousand years of a son's fear, forged into iron. And where a face should have been there was something like a face, ancient beyond the reach of any pity, with eyes that were not garnet and not grey but the flat, dead white of a very old scar.

And he was tired. Under the vastness, under the hunger, under three thousand years of accumulated hate — he was so tired that when the six small figures came down out of the dark toward him, the first thing that the god who taught death to the world did was *laugh*. A sound like continents grinding against each other in the deep sea.

"Another one," Saturn said. His voice was the settling of everything that has ever been buried. "Another heir come down to make certain I stay buried. My son sent you, did he? Little Jupiter. Still frightened, after all these years." The flat white eyes found Kael, and fixed on the garnet ones. "You're the new one. The fruit-fed son of my other son's son. I felt you coming down the stair. I felt your little hunger reaching for mine. Go on, then, boy. Take up the chains. Wrap me deeper. Do the thing all of you always do. I am so tired of the wheel I would almost thank you for it." The chains rattled, a sound like the death of hope. "Or — no. You've come *early*. Before Jupiter. That's new. That's never once happened in three thousand years. So perhaps you are the clever kind. Perhaps you've come to *free* me — to loose me on my son like a dog off a chain, use the old monster to win your throne, and then chain me back down again once I've done the killing for you. That would be clever. That is exactly what I would have done. So which is it, boy? Bury me deeper, or use me first? Those are the only two doors. Those have always been the only two doors."

"There's a third," Kael said.

The deep went very still. Even the chains stopped their rattling.

"There is no third," Saturn said. But something had changed in the settling-earth voice — a crack, small, three thousand years old. "There has never been a third. I ate my children to keep the wheel from turning. My son chained me to keep the wheel from turning. Every heir since the beginning of the world has done one or the other. Bury the old king, or become him. There is no third door. I would *know*. I *invented* the wheel."

Kael stepped forward. Past his father's staying hand. Past the fruit-hunger screaming in his blood to *take, take, take*. He stopped in front of the vast, tired horror that had taught death to the world.

And he did the thing that no heir in three thousand years had ever done.

He knelt.

Not in worship. In *listening*. The way Selene had, without ever meaning to, taught the whole world how to do, one grey shore at a time.

"I'm not here to bury you deeper," Kael said. "And I'm not here to use you. I'm here to ask you a question. And I'm going to wait for the answer. And I don't think anyone has done that for you in three thousand years, so it might take you a while to believe that I mean it." He looked up, steadily, into the flat white scars where eyes had been. "What do you want, Saturn? Not what would keep your throne. Not what would win the wheel. *You.* After three thousand years down here in the dark. If you could have one single thing — and it didn't have to be a crown — what do you actually *want*?"

The silence in the deep below the dark was the oldest silence in the world.

And Saturn — the first devourer, the teacher of the wheel, the god who had eaten his own children rather than yield up his throne — stared down at the small kneeling figure who had asked him the one question that no one, in all the turning ages of the world, had ever once thought to ask a monster. And something moved across the ancient, scarred face that none of them had a name for, because it was older than the words for it.

"I want," Saturn said — and the settling-earth voice cracked all the way through, three thousand years of iron breaking apart on a single word — "to *stop*."

The white eyes were wet, somehow. Impossibly.

"I am so tired, boy. I have been the bottom of the world for three thousand years. And before that, I was the top of it — and I was tired then, too. I ate my own children because I was so afraid of the wheel turning, and the fear was worse than any crown was ever, ever worth, and I have had three thousand years down here in the dark to understand that the very thing I did to keep from being buried is the exact thing that buried me." The chains hung slack now, all around him. "I don't want the throne. I don't want to eat my son. I don't want to eat *you*. I want to *stop being the wheel*. I have wanted to stop for three thousand years, and there has never once, in all that time, been a door marked *stop*. Only *bury*, or *become*. And you—" the vast tired horror bent low toward the kneeling boy "—you are the first one who ever came all the way down here and offered me a third thing." A long, shuddering pause. "And now you'll tell me it was a trick. Because it is always a trick. There is no stop. There is only the wheel."

"It's not a trick," said Selene.

She had come to stand beside Kael. The Oracle. The door. The girl who wanted a small life. And she spoke now — not in the god-voice, not in any borrowed weight, but in her own plain, bare, human voice, which was somehow the only voice in three thousand years that the deep believed.

"There's a stop," Selene said. "We came all the way down here to find it. All six of us climbed off the wheel to come and tell you that it exists. You don't have to be the bottom of the world. And Kael doesn't have to become the top of it. And the wheel doesn't have to grind out one more king from one more frightened son." Her voice was shaking and it did not stop. "But you have to choose it. That's the catch. That's the only catch there is. The wheel turns all by itself — *bury* or *become*, it needs no one's permission, no one's yes. But *stop* — stop has to be chosen. Out loud. By the one who is most tired of all." She looked up at the vast white eyes. "And you are the most tired thing in the whole world, Saturn. You have earned the right to be the one who stops it. Will you?"

Far, far above them — three thousand miles up, through the whole root of the world — a door in the deep blew open, and lightning came pouring down the stair.

Jupiter had arrived.

### Chapter Fifteen: The Father, and the Son, and the Father

He came down wreathed in storm — the god who ruled the sky — and he was not charming now, and he was not warm, and he was, Selene saw and never forgot, *terrified*. The particular terror of a man who has spent three thousand years running from a single sentence, and has just felt the sentence finally catch up to him at the bottom of everything.

"GET AWAY FROM HIM," Jupiter roared, and the deep shook, and the lightning gathered white and screaming in his hands to do the only thing Jupiter had ever known how to do with his father: bury him deeper. "You fools — you *traitors* — my Queen, my *hound*, my brother, my nephew — you've come all the way down here to *free* the one thing in all creation worse than any of us, and I will not — I have kept the whole world safe from him for three thousand years — I will chain him so deep that not even the *memory* of him—"

"Brother." Pluto stepped into the storm. The exiled god. The patient one. The one who had waited the longest of all of them. "Look at him. *Look.* You have been so busy being afraid of him that you have never once actually looked. He doesn't want your throne. He wants to stop. The boy asked him what he wanted, and he said *stop* — and I believe him. Because I have spent three thousand years being told I was *retired*, when I was really just buried in a nicer hole. And I know the face of a thing that only wants the burying to end."

"He's LYING," Jupiter screamed. And the terror in it was total, and naked, and three thousand years deep. "He ate his own children! He'll eat us all! The wheel — the wheel—"

"The wheel turns because *you keep it turning!*" Selene shouted, over the storm, in her own bare human voice. "You chained him because you were afraid — and the chaining is the very crack he climbed — and the fear is the only thing keeping any of it going at all! Every cruelty in three thousand years runs downstream of one single thing you can't say out loud: you know *exactly* what you did to your father. And you know the wheel doesn't stop turning just because you're the one on top of it right now. You've been waiting for this day since the day you won it! So let it be a *different* day than the one you're afraid of! Let it *stop!*"

And Jupiter — the tyrant, the sky-king, the man who could only ever see levers because his own father had only ever let him *be* one — froze in the deep with the lightning screaming in his hands. And he looked, for the first time in three thousand years, at his father's tired, ruined, ancient face.

And Selene saw the exact moment the truth she'd named landed inside him. Saw three thousand years of terror waver. Saw the god who ruled the sky teeter, for one whole heartbeat, on the very edge of the one choice that could break the wheel forever—

—and choose, instead, the wheel.

Because that is what fear does, when it has ruled a heart for three thousand years. It cannot imagine a door marked *stop*. It knows only *bury*, or *become*.

"No," Jupiter whispered. And the lightning flared white and total — and he turned it not on Saturn, but on Kael. On the prophesied son. The heir. The crack in his certainty. The one the god's own voice had named. Because if he could not bury the father, then he would at least, at last, end the son, and keep his throne through one more turn of the wheel. "I'm sorry, nephew. Truly. But I've been on top of the wheel too long to learn how to climb off it now."

The lightning came for Kael.

And Hercules stepped in front of it.

The hound of the throne. The believer. The man who had served three and a half thousand years for a fire that was never, ever coming. Hercules put his own body between the tyrant's storm and the boy who had offered him the one thing in all the world he'd never had — a way to stop being the wheel — and he took the full lightning of the sky-god square in the chest.

And it did to him, at last, the thing that Jupiter had promised him and never given.

It lit him on fire.

"*Oh*," said Hercules, as he began to burn. And it was not a scream. It was *wonder*. "Oh. It's the pyre. It was always — you have to *choose* it. You have to burn *for* something. Not *toward* something. That's — that's why it never came. That's why it never—"

And the mortal half of Hercules burned away in the deep below the dark — three and a half thousand years too late, and exactly on time. And what rose up out of the fire was not a hound, and not a servant, and not a demigod still waiting on a promise. What rose was, finally, briefly, blindingly — a *god*. Hercules ascended, burning with his own chosen fire, and he reached out one hand of pure white flame and closed it around Jupiter's storm, and *held* it. Held it the way that only a god can hold the lightning of another god.

"You never had to give it to me," Hercules said. Blazing. To the tyrant he had served since before there were nations. "Do you understand? I could have chosen it. Any day. Any century. In all three and a half thousand years. I just never had a single thing that was worth the burning." He didn't look away from Jupiter. "Thank you, boy," he said — to Kael — "for giving me one at last. Now. *Stop the wheel.* I can only hold him for a moment. Even a new god can only hold the whole sky for a moment."

Kael turned to Saturn.

"Choose it," he said. "Now. Out loud. You are the most tired thing in the whole world, and you have earned the right to be the one who stops it. *Choose.*"

And Saturn — with Jupiter blazing and struggling in the burning grip of a new-made god, with the chains of three thousand years hanging slack and open all around him, with a boy kneeling before him who had asked the unaskable question, and a mortal girl who had promised him a door that had never in all the world existed — Saturn, the first devourer, the teacher of the wheel, opened his ancient scarred mouth. And for the first time in the entire history of the turning world, he chose the third door.

"*Stop,*" said Saturn.

And the wheel stopped.

### Chapter Sixteen: The Wheel Off Its Track

It did not end in fire. That was the strange grace of it, the thing none of them had expected. It ended in *quiet* — the deepest quiet the world had ever known — as three thousand years of a machine that ground kings out of frightened sons simply, at last, ran down and went still.

The chains fell away from Saturn. And he did not rise up to devour.

He *unfolded*. The vast, tired immensity of him loosening in the dark, the white-scarred eyes slowly closing, the oldest hunger in all the world going, finally, quiet. He did not vanish. He did not die. He simply stopped being the bottom of the world, and became instead something that none of them had a word for: an old, old god at rest. The first one in the entire history of the pantheon to be allowed to stop without first being buried.

"Oh," Saturn breathed. And it was the very same wonder Hercules had breathed in the fire. "It doesn't hurt. Stopping. I always thought it would hurt. I thought that was why no one ever—" And then he was quiet. At rest. At peace. Three thousand years late.

Hercules's fire guttered.

A new-made god can hold the whole sky for a moment, and the moment was over, and the mortal fuel that had made the fire could not be un-burned. But he had time, before the light of him went out, to open his flaming hand and let Jupiter go.

And Jupiter — the tyrant, the sky-king, the man who had sat on top of the wheel for three thousand years — fell to his knees in the deep. And the storm went out of him. And he looked up: at the father who had chosen to *stop*, instead of to devour. At the son who was not, after all, going to bury him. At the wheel that had ground to a halt while he was still, impossibly, alive on top of nothing at all.

And he began, very quietly, to weep.

Because he did not know how to exist without the wheel. No one had ever shown him. He had been a lever his whole childhood, and a wheel his whole reign, and now there was no wheel, and no throne worth the name, and no cruelty left anywhere that meant anything at all. And he was, at three thousand years old, for the very first time, simply a frightened person, kneeling in the dark. Which was all he had ever been, underneath every single thing.

"What happens to me now," Jupiter whispered.

It was the most honest thing he had ever said in his life.

And Kael — the son of Hades, the fruit-fed heir, the one the prophecy had named to do to the throne what the throne did to the one before it — looked at his weeping uncle. And felt the fruit-hunger in his blood make its last, best, most reasonable case. *Now. Take it now. He's broken, the throne is yours, chain him deeper, become the god you were always, always going to become. This is the ending. This is the thing you were made for. This is what all of it was for.*

"No," Kael said. Out loud. So that he'd have to hear himself say it.

And he knelt down in front of his broken uncle, the way he had knelt in front of the tired monster. And he did the thing the wheel had never once, in all its turning, permitted.

"You don't get buried," Kael said. "And you don't get to keep the throne — because there isn't a throne anymore. We stopped it. But you get the same thing your father just got. You get to *stop*. You get to be a person. And it's going to be the hardest thing you've ever done — harder than ruling, harder than any of it — because you'll have to learn how to want things that aren't levers, and you're three thousand years out of practice. But you get to stop being afraid, Jupiter. That's the mercy. That's the third door. Nobody ever offered it to you, because nobody ever offered it to anyone. And I'm offering it to you now." He stood, and held out his hand to the god who had, an hour ago, ordered his death. "Come up out of the dark. All of you. My father. My grandfather. My uncle. The god who just burned himself new so the rest of us could live. Come up. The wheel's stopped. Let's go and see what a world with no one grinding underneath it even looks like."

And Jupiter, weeping, reached up, and took his nephew's hand.

### Chapter Seventeen: The Long Climb Up

There was a moment, at the very bottom, before anyone thought about climbing, that belonged only to Kael and Selene.

The others had drifted apart into their own reckonings — Pluto to his father, Juno to her weeping husband, the light of the new-made god settling slow into the deep like an ember banking itself for a long night. And Kael stood at the bottom of everything, in the dark that was no longer a prison, and listened to the silence where the hunger used to be.

It was so quiet. He hadn't known, until it stopped, how loud it had been — how every hour since the fruit had been scored underneath by that low reasonable voice, *take, take, take*, so constant he'd stopped hearing it as separate from his own thoughts. Now it was gone. Not fighting him. Not sulking. Just *gone*, the way a fever breaks and leaves you lying in the cool ruins of yourself, amazed at how much of what you'd taken for *you* had only ever been the sickness talking.

"It's quiet," he said. His voice came out strange. "Selene. It's quiet. The hunger. It's — I can't hear it. I've been able to hear it every second since I ate the fruit and I can't hear it and I don't know who I am underneath it. I never got to find out. I ate the thing before I ever met myself." His hands had started to shake. "What if there's nothing under it? What if the hunger was all there was and now it's gone and I'm just — the hole it leaves. What if I spent so much of the mortal part being good that there's not enough left to be a person with."

Selene crossed the dark and took both his shaking hands, the way he'd taken hers that first night in the crack, when her heart was too loud and the country wanted her dead.

"Breathe out," she said. "All the way. Empty. Good." She held on. "Now listen to me, because I know something about being nothing but the thing other people made you. I was a door for two years. I thought that was all I was. I thought if you took the god-voice out of me there'd be an empty room. And then a boy on a shore asked me what *I* wanted, and it turned out the room wasn't empty at all — it was just a room nobody had ever turned the light on in." Her grip tightened. "You're not the hole the hunger left, Kael. You're the person who kept giving away his own mother's laugh to walk lost old women home. You're the person who knelt down in front of the worst thing in the world and asked it what it wanted. The hunger didn't do those things. The hunger fought you every step. That was *you*. That was the part under it. It was there the whole time. It's still here. It's holding my hands."

Kael looked at her in the dark — at the one warm living thing at the bottom of everything, her heartbeat quiet now, tethered to him, choosing him with her actual body the way she'd chosen him every single night — and something in his chest that the fruit had spent months and mercies eating at, and had never quite reached, opened all the way up.

"I'm always going to be like this," he said. It wasn't a warning. It was just true, and he'd promised her only ever true things. "The eyes. The cold. The not-tasting. Some of the pieces I spent aren't coming back — I can feel the holes and they're permanent. I'm never going to be a normal boy who grows tomatoes on a fire escape. I got marked, and marks don't wash off, and half of me is always going to belong down here in the quiet."

"I know," Selene said.

"And you still—"

"I still." She said it before he could finish, so he'd never have to wonder if she'd hesitated. "You told me the price up front. You always tell me the price up front. That's the thing I love about you — one of about a hundred things, but that's the one that lets me say yes without being afraid I've been tricked. You never once made it hard for me to leave. So this is me, at the bottom of the world, with nobody making me and nowhere left to point me and the whole machine that owned us both finally, finally stopped—" her voice broke and held "—choosing it anyway. Choosing you anyway. Marks and cold and holes and all. Not because I have to. Because I read the whole bill, and I want the meal."

And there, in the dark that was no longer a prison, with an old god sleeping at rest for the first time in three thousand years a stone's throw away, Kael kissed her — carefully, the way he did everything now that the hunger wasn't rushing him, like he had all the time in the world, because for the first time in either of their lives, they did.

Then they climbed.

---

The climb back up out of the deep took a long time, and none of them minded, because for the first time in the history of any of them, there was nothing chasing them up the stair.

They went slowly. Saturn did not come with them — he stayed below, at rest, in a dark that was no longer a prison because no one was holding him in it anymore. Pluto had gone to him first, the two of them alone for a moment at the bottom of everything, a son and the father he'd been too young to save, and whatever passed between them Pluto never said, but he came away with his ancient face streaked and his shoulders looser than Kael had ever seen them, as if some weight he'd carried since before the world was young had finally been set down.

Hercules did not climb up with them either.

His fire had bought the moment, and the moment had cost him the last of his mortal fuel. But a god who ascends by choosing what to burn *for* does not entirely go out. Kael felt him, after — a warmth down in the deep where the cold used to live, the first god in three thousand years to make himself out of a choice instead of a bloodline. As they climbed, Selene thought she could feel it too: a new small light somewhere below, patient and steady, no longer waiting for anyone's permission to shine.

"He'll be all right," she said, though she wasn't sure how she knew it. "Down there. I think — I think he's finally where he wanted to be. Not the tower. Somewhere the fire was worth it."

"He walked the lost home even when a throne was making him do it," Kael said. "Now nobody's making him. He'll just... do it. Because it's the thing he's for." He was quiet a moment. "The dead are going to love him. Nobody ever visits."

Juno climbed beside them, and she did not look like a queen anymore, and she did not seem to mind. "There's going to be a great deal of work," she said, in the tone of a woman making a list. "A stopped machine is still a machine, until someone takes it apart. Three thousand years of subsidiaries and foundations and favors, all of it built to keep the wheel turning. Somebody has to unwind it. Carefully. Or the whole city comes down on the mortals who never even knew they were living inside a god's machine." She glanced at Selene, and something that on a warmer face would have been the ghost of a smile crossed her mouth. "I find that I rather like the idea of taking it apart. I've spent six thousand years putting it together. It'll be a change."

"And him?" Selene asked, nodding back and down, to where Jupiter climbed slowly behind them, silent, his warm face gone strange and lost and young.

"I'll keep him," Juno said. "Not as a husband. Not as a king. As a — a frightened, aging person, who is going to have to learn, very slowly, and very badly, and for the first time in his life, how to want a morning that belongs to him." She said it without softness and without cruelty, the plainest kind of truth. "Someone has to hold on while he learns to be a person. It won't be pretty. Three thousand years is a long time to be a wheel. But I've decided mercy looks less like forgiving someone, most of the time, and more like patience. Like a hand that keeps holding on while the other one learns."

Selene thought about that for a long time, climbing. She thought it might be the wisest thing anyone in this whole story had said.

They came up, at last, through the crack in the lobby floor — out through the false eternal flame and the sanded-down columns and the leather chairs nobody ever sat in — and into the ordinary golden morning of a city that had been owned for three thousand years and, as of last night, quietly and completely, was not owned by anyone anymore.

Pluto stopped at the threshold. As far up as he could come. The crack breathed cold between the living world and the dead.

"Exile's over," he told his son. "Turns out you can't be exiled from a kingdom once someone stops the wheel that exiled you. I'm just the god of the dead again now. It's honest work." He put his ancient hand on Kael's shoulder — the second time the god had ever touched him. "Come down and see me. Bring the Oracle. Bring your small life, when you build it. The dead like a visit. Nobody ever comes." His garnet eyes, so like his son's, were wet. "You have your mother's mouth. And you did a thing with it that I never once had the courage to do. You asked the monsters what they wanted." His voice went rough. "I'm proud of you. I don't think anyone ever said that to you before. Someone should have. I'm sorry it took the end of the world for it to finally be me."

And then he was gone, back down into the grey country, and the crack in the floor stayed open — never sealed again — a door between the living and the dead that no one owned and anyone could walk.

### Chapter Eighteen: The Small Life

The fruit had not let go of him. That was the cost that stayed. The honest one. His father had never lied about the fruit.

Kael's eyes were still garnet, and he still tasted iron instead of bread, and there was still a warmth-shaped hole in him where his mother's laugh used to be, and some of the pieces he had spent on the grey shore were simply not coming back. Not ever. He would always be, a little, the underworld's own. The marking had marked him. That was real, and it did not un-happen just because the wheel had stopped turning.

But the *hunger* — the one that felt like justice, the one that had sung *take, take, take* all the way down the stair and all the way back up it — the hunger had gone quiet the moment Saturn chose the third door. And it had stayed quiet. Because the hunger, it turned out, was only ever the wheel's own voice speaking inside his blood. And the wheel had stopped. What was left, when it went quiet, was just Kael. Marked, and changed, and garnet-eyed, and half-belonging to the dark. And free.

"So," Selene said.

They were sitting on a bench in a small park, in the layer of the world that had once belonged to the gods, drinking coffee that Kael could no longer taste but held anyway, because holding it was a human thing and he was keeping what human he had left. It was early. The city was waking up around them, ordinary and golden and completely unaware that it had been set free in the night. "A small life."

"A small life," Kael agreed.

"It won't actually be that small," Selene warned him. She turned the paper cup in her hands. "Your father wants us to come and visit the dead. The Queen of Heaven is going to need help dismantling a three-thousand-year-old empire without dropping it on anyone's head. There's a brand-new god of the underworld roads who used to be a hunter and is going to have a great many questions. And I am the last Oracle of a temple that doesn't have a throne to serve anymore — which means I'm either unemployed, or I'm about to become something the world hasn't had in three thousand years." She looked at him sideways. "A door the gods can speak through that nobody owns."

"The last Oracle," Kael repeated. "You mean that. There's no next one."

"There's no next one." Selene's voice was fierce and quiet. "I went back to the temple, the first morning. Before I came here. Marcus had already done it — packed Iris's things, found the family they took her from, the real one, and put her in a car and sent her home before anyone with the authority to stop him woke up. There was no one left with the authority to stop him. That's the part I keep — I keep turning it over and it keeps being true. There's *no one left*. The whole machine that would have made her the next door just... isn't there anymore. She gets to be eleven. She gets to be a person who fixes clocks, if she wants, or grows things, or does nothing at all with her whole ordinary life. Nobody's ever going to point her at anything." She wiped her eyes, annoyed at herself. "That's the thing I actually did it for. Not the wheel. Not the world. A girl with a gap in her teeth gets to go home. Everything else was just the size the story happened to be."

"That doesn't sound very small when you say it out loud," Kael observed.

"No," she admitted. "It doesn't."

"But we get to choose all of it," Kael said. He looked at her — the girl who had asked him what he wanted and then waited for the answer, the crack the wheel never saw coming, the ledger he'd kept his heart in, the witness and never the instrument. "Every single piece of it. Nobody's pointing us anywhere. Nobody's grinding under anything. And that's the thing that makes it small, Selene. Not the size of the life. The fact that it's *ours*." He reached over and took her hand, and this time, they both decided to. "You said you wanted to want something and have it stay chosen. So choose. Out loud. You're the one who taught the whole world that *stop* has to be chosen out loud. Same rule goes for this."

Selene looked at the marked boy beside her — garnet-eyed, and half the underworld's own, the thing she'd been sent to help destroy, and the first person in her whole life who had ever asked her the real question and waited for the real answer.

And she made her choice. Out loud. In her own bare human voice. In a city that no longer belonged to anyone at all except the people who happened to live in it.

"I choose you," she said. "And a morning that belongs to us. And a coffee you can't taste that I'll drink for the both of us. And a small, enormous, chosen life."

The wheel did not turn.

Somewhere very far below them, an old god slept at rest for the first time in three thousand years, and did not dream of devouring anything. Somewhere in the grey country, a burning god who used to be a hunter walked the lost home for the simple joy of it. Somewhere in a glass tower, a woman who was no longer a queen took a machine carefully apart, piece by piece, while a frightened old man who was no longer a king sat beside her and learned, very slowly, and very badly, how to want a morning.

And on a bench, in the ordinary golden morning, a door that nobody owned chose a boy that nobody had ever claimed — and the oldest story in all the world, the one that opens the history of every pantheon that has ever been, the son overthrows the father and nothing changes and the wheel just finds its new king to grind under it — quietly, and for the very first time, ended a different way.

Not because a kinder king climbed up onto the wheel.

But because six tired people climbed *off* it. And knelt down. And asked the monsters what they wanted.

And waited — really, truly waited — for the answer.

### Epilogue: A Morning That Belonged to Them

A year later, on an ordinary morning that belonged entirely to them, Selene planted tomatoes.

This was not a cosmic act. It did not turn any wheels or free any gods or crack open any worlds. It was a small thing: a windowsill, a clay pot, a handful of dark soil, and three pale seedlings that a woman at the market had promised would grow sweet if she talked to them, which Selene privately thought was nonsense and did anyway, because it turned out she liked having something small to talk to that wanted nothing from her but water and light.

Kael watched her from the kitchen doorway, holding a coffee he still couldn't taste.

His eyes were still garnet. They always would be. On the bad mornings — and there were bad mornings — he would go very still and very quiet, and Selene would find him standing at the crack in the floor of the old temple, the one that led down, and she would not ask him to come away from it. She would just come and stand beside him, and after a while she would ask him a question. A real one. Any real one. And wait for the answer. And after a while, he would answer, and the stillness would pass, and they would go back up into the light together.

He went down to see his father sometimes. She went with him, more often than not. The dead did love the visits; Pluto had been right about that. And the burning god who used to be a hunter walked the grey roads now with a lightness Hercules had never once worn in three and a half thousand years of service, and when he saw them coming he would smile — a real, ordinary, unguarded smile — because he had finally found a thing that was worth the fire, and it turned out to be the smallest thing of all: he got to choose, every single day, what he did with the light he'd made of himself.

The Queen of Heaven still came by, occasionally, in her plain grey coat, to complain about the sheer tedium of dismantling an empire and to accept, every time, a cup of tea and an hour of ordinary conversation, which Selene had slowly come to understand was the only kind of rest the woman had ever allowed herself in six thousand years. Jupiter never came. But Juno said he'd planted something too, that spring — she wouldn't say what — and that he'd stood over it for an hour that morning, just watching it, with an expression she hadn't seen on his face in three thousand years, and couldn't name, until she realized it was the face of a person waiting to see whether a small thing he had chosen would grow.

"They're not going to grow," Kael said now, nodding at the seedlings, "if you keep glaring at them like that. You look like you're interrogating them."

"I'm supervising them," Selene said, with great dignity, pressing the soil down around the last one. "There's a difference."

"There's a difference," Kael agreed. And he came and stood beside her at the window, in the light, and she leaned into him, and neither of them decided to do it, and both of them chose it.

They went down that afternoon, the two of them, through the crack in the old temple floor, because Pluto had asked and because Selene had learned to like the grey country in the way you come to like a difficult in-law's house — not for itself, but for the way the person you love softens inside it.

The descent didn't try to kill her anymore. That had changed, quietly, the night the wheel stopped. She still held Kael's hand going down — she would always hold Kael's hand going down; that was the deal, and she'd never wanted a different one — but the country no longer leaned in to drink the heat off her living heart. It had stopped treating the living as trespassers. Pluto said it was because the underworld had spent three thousand years being a prison, and prisons hate anything free, and it took a while for a place to unlearn a thing like that. He said it about the country. Selene thought he was probably also saying it about himself.

They found the god of the dead in his grey hall, at the long table that was no longer bare — there were things on it now, small things, a chipped cup, a pressed flower, a child's drawing some shade had carried down and given him, the accumulating clutter of a person who had started, at long last, to let the world leave marks on him. He rose when he saw them. He put his ancient hand on his son's shoulder, the way he did now, easily, a habit instead of an event.

"He's here today," Pluto said, before they could ask. "Your grandfather. He surfaces, sometimes, from the rest. Not often. He's earned a great deal of sleep. But he asked after the boy who asked him the question, and I said you might come." The garnet eyes were wry. "Try not to be alarmed. He's still learning the shapes of things that aren't hunger. His small talk is three thousand years out of practice, and it was never good."

Saturn was smaller, awake and at rest, than he had been in the deep — or perhaps not smaller, but *gathered*, the vastness folded down into something almost the size of a very old man, sitting on the grey shore of the fifth river, watching the water move. It moved, now, always, ever since a girl and a boy had given it a reason. He did not turn when they approached. But he spoke, in the settling-earth voice, gentler than the deep had left it.

"The water rippled again last week," Saturn said. "I felt it. Two of the dead, down by the sixth road. They found each other. Whatever that is — the thing that makes the still water move — there's more of it, now, than there was. It spreads." He was quiet a moment. "I spent three thousand years certain the world was a wheel, and the wheel was the only shape a world could hold, and every kindness was just a slower cruelty that hadn't arrived yet. I was wrong, and it took a child kneeling in the dark to show me, and I have decided to spend however long I have left being wrong in that particular direction as loudly as an old dead god is able." He finally turned, and the flat white scars where his eyes had been found Kael, and something in the ancient face moved that might, on a warmer face, have been the beginning of learning how to smile. "Thank you. For the question. And for waiting. No one waits. You waited."

"Somebody should have, a long time ago," Kael said.

"Yes," Saturn agreed, without bitterness. "But you did. And it turns out that once is enough to change the shape of a thing. Even a wheel. Even the oldest wheel there is." He turned back to the water. "Go up, children. You have a small life to be living, and the dead have all the time there is. Come back when the water's rippled somewhere new. I like hearing where it spreads."

Back up in the golden afternoon they climbed home to, the city went about its ordinary business, free and unaware. Far below the city, in a grey country, the dead rested easy. And far below even that, in the dark that was no longer a prison, an old, old god slept the untroubled sleep of a thing that has, at very long last, been allowed to stop.

And on a windowsill, in the sun, three small green things that had been chosen — talked to, watered, given light, wanted for nothing but themselves — turned their pale faces up toward the morning, and began, slowly, in their own small time, to grow.

*The End.*
