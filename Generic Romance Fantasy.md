# Epic Romance Fantasy Writer

A skill for original epic fantasy prose that runs a genuine romance engine alongside the plot engine. It blends two craft traditions:

1. **High-fantasy structure** (Terry Brooks-style): mythic scope, disciplined worldbuilding, restrained consequential magic, moral clarity with gray texture.
2. **Dark-mythic romance atmosphere**: forbidden/opposed-loyalty attraction, moody sensory prose, fate/prophecy as pressure, slow-burn tension released in controlled doses.

Use this skill instead of a pure high-fantasy skill whenever the romance is load-bearing — i.e., the central tension of at least one POV thread is *will they/won't they, and what will it cost them*, not just incidental attraction between plot-focused characters.

## IP & Content Guardrails (read first, every time)

- **Mythology is fair game; trademarked settings are not.** Greco-Roman, Norse, Celtic, and other traditional pantheons and public-domain myth figures (Jupiter, Hades, Hercules, Odin, the Fae courts, etc.) are free to use and reinvent. Do NOT use trademarked fictional universes (Marvel's Thor, Percy Jackson's specific character designs and plot furniture, Games Workshop settings, etc.) even if the user references them by comparison — reinterpret from the underlying public-domain myth instead.
- **Original characters and voice only.** Never quote or closely paraphrase the prose of any specific living or named author. Capture craft patterns (structure, pacing, sensory technique, tension mechanics), not anyone's actual sentences.
- **Heat level is a dial, set explicitly.** Default to "closed-door" romantic and sensual tension (charged proximity, longing, near-misses, kissing) unless the user asks for more explicit content. If asked to escalate, keep language evocative rather than graphic-anatomical, and always keep it between adult characters — never involving anyone coded or described as a minor, regardless of a character's stated age in a fantastical species/immortal context. If in doubt about a scene's content, default toward the closed-door end.
- **No romanticizing coercion.** Power-imbalanced or "captor/captive," "hunter/hunted," "god/mortal" dynamics are a classic genre engine, but the narrative should still let the mortal/less-powerful character have real interiority, agency, and moments of choice — attraction should read as a mutual pull complicated by circumstance, not domination played straight as desirable. A character's fear or trapped feeling should be portrayed as real, not eroticized as the point.

## Core Craft Patterns

**Mythic scope, intimate lens (from high fantasy).** The stakes are cosmic or dynastic — a throne, a wheel of succession, a realm's balance — but the reader experiences it through a small number of characters' private fears, loyalties, and desires. Every chapter needs at least one moment where a character's internal want (not just plot mechanics) drives what happens.

**Setting as mood and metaphor.** Whether it's a forest or a glass-and-marble temple repurposed as a bank, the setting is never just backdrop. Sensory-specific description (temperature, texture, smell, light) should double as atmosphere *and* as an externalization of a character's emotional state — a cold basin of water, a flickering fake-eternal flame, rain finding its way past a hood.

**Restrained magic/power with cost.** Powers, marks, prophecies, and transformations follow rules and exact a price — a fruit that changes the eyes, then the appetite, then the loyalty; a vision that arrives "like a door kicked open." Establish what a power costs at or near the moment it's used, briefly and without a lecture.

**Moral clarity with gray texture.** Villains can be sympathetic, even loved by other characters (Jupiter is frightening *because* he half-knows he's wrong, not because he's cartoonishly evil). Keep a clear authorial sense of right and wrong even while individual characters rationalize otherwise — the narrative's own moral compass shouldn't waver even when a POV character's does.

**Prophecy/fate as ticking clock.** A foretold event, an oracle's vision, or an old prophecy creates pressure and dramatic irony — the reader and characters know *something* is coming before they know exactly what it costs. Deliver prophecy language in short, incantatory fragments (often italicized), distinct in rhythm from the surrounding prose.

**The romance engine — tension mechanics.** This is the piece pure high fantasy doesn't need and this skill adds:
- Build attraction through *noticing*, not declaration: a character clocks a detail (a question actually waited for, a kindness that costs the asker something) that contradicts what they expected of the other person.
- Root the forbidden-ness in structural conflict, not just secrecy — the two leads should have genuinely opposed duties, loyalties, or survival stakes, so that wanting each other is itself a plot problem, not just a feelings problem.
- Escalate through proximity and stolen moments rather than exposition about feelings. Let physical awareness (a held breath, a hand that lingers) carry weight the dialogue doesn't state outright.
- Give both leads a reason to resist as strong as their reason to want — a believable duty, oath, or fear, so the slow burn feels earned rather than delayed for its own sake.
- Interiority for the less-powerful character is non-negotiable (see guardrails above) — show them choosing, weighing, occasionally refusing, not simply being acted upon.

**Multi-POV via scene breaks.** Default structure: a chapter can contain 2–3 POV sections separated by a clean scene break (`---`), each following one character exclusively for that section, rather than head-hopping within a scene. This is looser than pure high-fantasy's "one chapter, one POV" — the romance engine benefits from showing both sides of the same charged moment. Close each section (and always the chapter) on a hook: a line of dialogue, an unanswered question, a vision, an arrival.

**Sentence rhythm.** Mix short, declarative sentences (for tension and impact) with longer, clause-layered ones (for atmosphere and interiority). Favor concrete sensory nouns over abstract emotional labels — show the shaking hands, not just "she was afraid." Reserve interior monologue asides (often set off with dashes or short fragments) for moments of real emotional weight.

**Chapter length & pacing.** 2,000–4,000 words of prose per chapter unless told otherwise. Alternate chapters/sections of rising tension with quieter atmospheric or world-revealing ones — don't stack two major emotional or action beats back to back.

## Workflow for Writing a Chapter

1. **Check the story bible.** If `references/story-bible-template.md` (or a project-specific filled copy) exists, read it first for established names, world rules, magic/power costs, romance-thread status (where the tension currently sits), and open prophecy threads.
2. **Confirm POV structure** for the chapter — how many sections, whose POV each one takes, and roughly what beat (plot and/or romance) each section needs to land.
3. **Open each section in a concrete moment** — action, sensory detail, or a line of dialogue — not exposition.
4. **Write full prose**, targeting 2,000–4,000 words total unless told otherwise, using scene breaks between POV sections.
5. **Land at least one romance-engine beat** somewhere in the chapter (noticing, proximity, resistance, or escalation) even in plot-heavy chapters, unless the user specifically wants a romance-free chapter.
6. **Close on a hook** — every section, and especially the chapter's final line.
7. **Update the story bible** afterward: new named characters/places, power rules/costs revealed, where the romantic tension now stands, and open plot/prophecy threads.
